Most firms adopted e-signature tools for one reason: speed. A caller who gets a retainer on their phone while still on the line is far less likely to sign with the next firm they dial. But **law firm e-signature compliance** is rarely reviewed after the tool is installed, and that gap shows up at the worst moment, when a client disputes a fee, claims they never agreed to terms, or a bar investigator asks for the signed agreement.

An electronic signature is legally valid in most situations. Whether *your* electronic signature is provable is a separate question, and it depends on process, not software.

## What Makes an Electronic Signature Legally Valid

Two statutes do most of the work. The federal ESIGN Act (15 U.S.C. 7001 and following) and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), adopted in nearly every state, establish that a signature or contract cannot be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic. New York uses its own Electronic Signatures and Records Act with similar effect.

Both frameworks rest on the same core elements:

- **Intent to sign.** The signer must take a deliberate action indicating assent, such as clicking an adopt-and-sign button.
- **Consent to do business electronically.** The parties must have agreed, expressly or by conduct, to transact electronically.
- **Association of signature with the record.** The signature must be logically attached to the specific document it approves.
- **Record retention.** The signed record must be kept in a form that can be accurately reproduced later.

Notice what is absent: no statute requires a particular vendor, a drawn signature, or a certificate. A typed name can qualify. The legal weight comes from the evidence surrounding the act.

## Law Firm E-Signature Compliance and the Contingency Fee Rule

Attorney fee agreements carry rules that ordinary commercial contracts do not. Under [ABA Model Rule 1.5(c)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_5_fees/), a contingent fee agreement must be in a writing signed by the client, stating how the fee is determined, the percentage, how expenses are handled, and whether they come out before or after the fee is calculated.

States adopt their own versions, and some are stricter on timing, language, or required disclosures. Electronic signatures generally satisfy a "signed writing" requirement under ESIGN and UETA, but you should confirm your state's bar guidance before assuming so for every document type.

This is why e-signature compliance is a bar-rules issue and not only a contract-law issue. A defective retainer can expose a firm to fee forfeiture or discipline, not just an unenforceable clause.

## Documents That Electronic Signatures May Not Cover

ESIGN and UETA carve out categories where electronic records or signatures may not suffice. The exact list differs by state, but the common exclusions include:

1. Wills, codicils, and testamentary trusts.
2. Certain family law matters, including adoption and divorce, under ESIGN's exclusions.
3. Court orders, notices, and official court documents.
4. Specific notices such as default or foreclosure on a primary residence, or cancellation of health or life insurance benefits.

For estate planning, family law, and real estate practices, this matters. A firm that runs every engagement through the same remote-signing flow may be sending clients documents that need wet ink or a witnessed process.

## Law Firm E-Signature Compliance Starts With the Audit Trail

If a client later says "that's not my signature," the document alone will not save you. The audit trail will. A defensible signing record should capture:

- The signer's name, email address, or phone number used to deliver the document.
- Timestamps for sent, viewed, and signed events.
- The IP address and device information at signing.
- The exact version of the document presented, preserved unaltered.
- The consent-to-electronic-process disclosure the signer accepted.

Most reputable e-signature platforms produce a completion certificate with these details. The mistake firms make is filing only the signed PDF and discarding the certificate, or letting a trial subscription lapse and losing access to the logs.

This is the same logic behind [lead consent records](/blog/law-firm-lead-consent-records): the evidence has to outlive the vendor relationship and be retrievable years later.

## Where E-Signature Workflows Break Down at Intake

The compliance failures we see most often are process failures, not technology failures.

### Signing before conflicts clear

Sending a retainer to a caller before a conflict check is complete creates an agreement the firm may have to unwind. Sequence matters, and the same discipline applies to [prospective client confidentiality](/blog/law-firm-prospective-client-confidentiality) duties that begin before any signature.

### Text-to-sign links without consent

Delivering signing links by SMS pulls in messaging rules on top of signature rules. If your intake team texts a retainer link, carrier and consent requirements apply, as covered in our guide to [law firm text message compliance](/blog/law-firm-text-message-compliance).

### No explanation of the fee terms

A client who clicks "sign" without a conversation about the percentage and costs has technically signed. They have also given you a credible argument that they were never informed. Pair every electronic retainer with a documented verbal walkthrough, and note it in the file.

### Shared devices and email accounts

When a spouse's email or a family phone receives the link, you cannot show who actually signed. For higher-value matters, consider a verification step such as an access code or ID check.

## Retention, Storage, and Retrieval

Signing is the beginning of the obligation. Firms must retain the executed agreement and its audit data for the period their state requires, which for client files commonly runs several years after the matter closes. Confirm your jurisdiction's rule and fold it into your [legal data retention policy](/blog/legal-data-retention-policy).

Practical safeguards:

- Store the signed document and completion certificate together in your case management system, not only inside the e-signature vendor's portal.
- Test retrieval annually by pulling a random signed retainer and its certificate.
- Verify that the stored copy is accurate and unaltered, since both ESIGN and UETA expect records to be reproducible.

The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has consistently shown that clients expect digital convenience from their lawyers, which is exactly why this infrastructure deserves the same rigor as the rest of your intake stack.

## A Compliance Checklist for Your Retainer Workflow

Run this against your current process:

1. Confirm your state's bar rules on electronic fee agreements and contingent fee writing requirements.
2. Add an electronic-transaction consent disclosure ahead of the signature step.
3. Exclude document types that ESIGN or your state's UETA does not cover.
4. Require conflict clearance before any retainer is sent.
5. Document the verbal fee explanation in the client file.
6. Archive the signed document and the audit certificate together.
7. Review the full flow alongside your [client onboarding process](/blog/law-firm-client-onboarding-process), since signature friction and signature risk usually live in the same step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are electronic signatures legally binding on law firm retainer agreements?

Yes, electronic signatures are generally binding on retainer agreements under the federal ESIGN Act and state UETA laws. The signature must show intent to sign, be tied to the document, and be retained in a reproducible form. Because bar rules govern fee agreements, confirm your state's requirements for contingent fee writings as well.

### Does a typed name count as a valid e-signature for a law firm?

A typed name can count as a valid e-signature if the signer intended it as their signature and consented to transact electronically. No statute requires a drawn or handwritten-style signature. The strength of the signature depends on the audit trail showing who signed, when, and which document version they saw.

### What documents cannot be signed electronically under ESIGN and UETA?

Wills, codicils, and testamentary trusts are the most common exclusions, along with certain family law matters, court orders and notices, and specific consumer notices such as foreclosure or insurance cancellation. Exact exclusions vary by state, so check your jurisdiction before routing any document through a remote signing flow.

### How long should a law firm keep e-signature audit records?

Keep audit records for at least as long as you must retain the underlying client file, which many states set at several years after the matter closes. Store the completion certificate with the signed agreement in your own system so it remains retrievable if you change e-signature vendors.
