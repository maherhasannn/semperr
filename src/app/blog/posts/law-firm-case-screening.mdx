Most firms treat intake as a funnel to widen: more calls, more consultations, more signed retainers. But the firms with the best margins do something that feels backwards. They get faster and more disciplined at saying no.

Law firm case screening is the process of deciding, early and consistently, which matters deserve attorney time. Without it, marginal cases absorb hours of review, drag down your signed-case quality, and push out the matters that actually pay. This article breaks down how to build a screening process that is fast, defensible, and fair to the people you decline.

## Why Law Firm Case Screening Is a Growth Lever, Not a Gatekeeping Chore

Capacity is the real constraint in a contingency or flat-fee practice. An attorney who spends three hours reviewing a case that was never viable has not just wasted three hours. That attorney also delayed a response to a strong lead who signed with a competitor.

Poor screening shows up in predictable places:

- Consultations that end with "we cannot help you" after 30 minutes of attorney time
- Cases signed, worked for months, then dropped when a fatal problem surfaces
- Intake staff who escalate everything because nobody told them where the line is
- Paid leads blamed for low quality when the real issue is unclear acceptance standards

The cost compounds. If you already track [law firm cost per case](/blog/law-firm-cost-per-case), add the internal review hours spent on cases you never sign. Many firms find that hidden cost rivals their media spend.

## Define Case Acceptance Criteria Before You Take the Call

You cannot screen consistently against a standard nobody wrote down. Case acceptance criteria are the written, practice-area-specific rules that tell intake staff what qualifies, what disqualifies, and what needs attorney review.

### Hard disqualifiers

These are facts that end the conversation. They should be checkable in the first few minutes of a call:

- Statute of limitations has run or is within a window you cannot work
- Wrong jurisdiction or a state where no attorney at the firm is licensed
- Adverse party is a client or a related entity (a conflict)
- Claimed damages fall below your minimum economic threshold
- The prospect already has counsel on the same matter

### Qualifying factors

These raise or lower priority without ending the call. In a personal injury practice they might include liability clarity, treatment status, insurance coverage, and documented damages. In employment or family law they will differ, but the principle holds: write them down.

### Attorney-review triggers

Define the narrow set of situations where intake should not decide alone, such as novel legal theories, high-value claims, or sensitive facts. Everything else should be resolvable by trained staff. If your intake team escalates more than a small share of calls, your criteria are too vague.

## Build a Screening Scorecard Your Intake Team Can Run in Five Minutes

A scorecard turns judgment into a repeatable process. It does not replace attorney discretion, but it stops the same lead from getting accepted by one person and declined by another.

A workable structure:

1. **Pass or fail on hard disqualifiers.** Any fail ends the screen.
2. **Score the qualifying factors.** Use a simple three-tier scale rather than a 100-point model nobody trusts.
3. **Assign a disposition.** Accept for consultation, route to attorney review, decline with referral, or decline outright.
4. **Log the reason.** Every decline gets a coded reason in your CRM.

That last step is where most firms fall short. Coded decline reasons are data. After a quarter you will know whether you are declining mostly for limitations problems (a marketing timing issue), damages thresholds (a targeting issue), or conflicts (a process issue).

If you already use a scoring framework, this pairs directly with [law firm lead scoring](/blog/law-firm-lead-scoring), which prioritizes leads by likelihood to convert. Screening answers a different question: should we take this at all?

## Make Law Firm Case Screening Fast With the Right Intake Questions

Speed matters on both sides. A prospect who needs a yes or no should not wait two days for it, and a viable lead should not sit behind a stack of unqualified ones.

Order your questions so disqualifiers come first. Ask date of incident, location, and whether they have an attorney before you ask for a narrative. A well-built [law firm intake script](/blog/law-firm-intake-script) does this by default, front-loading the facts that can end a screen and saving open-ended storytelling for prospects who pass.

Two practical rules help:

- **Never let the caller tell the whole story before you check limitations and conflicts.** It wastes their time and creates confidentiality obligations you may not want.
- **Have a decision deadline.** Set an internal standard, such as a disposition within one business day of first contact, and measure against it.

For the leads that arrive from paid sources, screening quality depends heavily on what you buy. Leads built on [high-intent legal leads](/blog/high-intent-legal-leads) signals arrive with fewer disqualifiers, which shortens every downstream step.

## Handle Declined Cases Without Creating Liability

Declining is not the end of your obligations. How you say no determines whether you create a malpractice exposure, a bad review, or a goodwill referral source.

### Send a non-engagement letter every time

A non-engagement letter states in plain language that you are not representing the person, that you have not evaluated the merits in full, and that deadlines may apply. It should flag the statute of limitations risk without giving legal advice about the specific date, and it should urge the person to consult another attorney promptly.

Sent consistently, this letter is one of the cheapest risk controls a firm has. Sent inconsistently, it creates a record that you treated similar prospects differently.

### Understand what the prospect told you

Information shared during a screen can trigger duties under [ABA Model Rule 1.18](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_18_duties_to_prospective_client/), which governs duties to prospective clients, even if no engagement follows. That is another reason to ask limiting questions first. Our guide to [prospective client confidentiality](/blog/law-firm-prospective-client-confidentiality) covers how to structure intake so you collect what you need and nothing more.

### Refer out deliberately

Declined does not have to mean abandoned. A short list of vetted firms for matters outside your practice turns a dead end into a service, and it can build the reciprocal relationships described in our piece on [attorney referral marketing](/blog/attorney-referral-marketing). Follow your jurisdiction's rules on referral fees and disclosures before you formalize any arrangement.

## Measure Whether Your Screening Is Working

A screening process you never measure will drift. Track a small set of numbers monthly:

- **Screen-to-consult rate:** share of screened leads who reach an attorney consultation
- **Consult-to-sign rate:** if this is low, your screen is too loose
- **Decline rate by reason:** the heart of the system
- **Time to disposition:** hours from first contact to a final yes or no
- **Post-signing drop rate:** cases accepted and later dropped, a direct signal of missed disqualifiers

The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has consistently shown that responsiveness and intake experience shape which firm a consumer hires. Strict screening should never mean slow screening. The goal is a fast, courteous no and a fast, confident yes.

Review decline reasons with your marketing team quarterly. If one source produces a high share of limitations declines, that is a media problem you can fix at the source rather than absorb at intake.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is law firm case screening?

Law firm case screening is the process of evaluating a prospective matter against written criteria to decide whether the firm should accept, refer, or decline it. It typically covers jurisdiction, limitations deadlines, conflicts, damages, and case value. A structured screen lets intake staff make most decisions without attorney involvement.

### What are good case acceptance criteria for a law firm?

Good case acceptance criteria are written, practice-area-specific, and split into hard disqualifiers, qualifying factors, and attorney-review triggers. Hard disqualifiers include expired limitations periods, conflicts, and wrong jurisdiction. Qualifying factors, such as liability strength and documented damages, determine priority.

### Do I need to send a non-engagement letter when I decline a case?

Yes, sending a non-engagement letter is the standard best practice whenever you decline a prospective client. It confirms in writing that no attorney-client relationship exists and warns that time limits may apply. Sending it consistently to every declined prospect reduces malpractice exposure.

### How long should it take to screen a new legal lead?

A first-pass screen should take roughly five minutes, with a final disposition within one business day. Hard disqualifiers such as limitations, jurisdiction, and conflicts can be checked early in the call. Slower timelines cost you viable leads who sign with faster competitors.

### Can intake staff decline cases, or must an attorney decide?

Trained intake staff can generally decline cases that fail written hard disqualifiers, while an attorney should make the call on borderline or high-value matters. Check your jurisdiction's rules on supervising nonlawyer staff. Document the criteria so declines are consistent and defensible.
