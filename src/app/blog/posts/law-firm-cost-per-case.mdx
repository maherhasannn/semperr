Most law firms track their marketing spend. Very few track what that spend actually buys them per case. The gap between those two metrics — total budget versus **law firm cost per case** — is where practices either build a scalable acquisition engine or quietly bleed margin on channels that look productive but aren't.

Getting this number right isn't just accounting hygiene. It's the single most important calculation a managing partner can make before signing a new marketing contract, allocating next quarter's budget, or deciding whether to double down on a channel generating volume but not value.

## What "Law Firm Cost Per Case" Actually Means

Cost per case (CPC) is total marketing and acquisition spend divided by the number of cases signed in a given period. That sounds simple. In practice, most firms undercount both the numerator and the denominator in ways that distort the metric entirely.

**The formula:**

Cost Per Case = Total Acquisition Spend ÷ Cases Signed

The denominator should be *signed retainers* — not leads, not consultations, not "interested prospects." Until a client signs, you haven't acquired a case. You've incurred a cost.

The numerator should include everything that moved a stranger toward becoming a client: paid media, agency fees, lead vendor costs, intake staff salaries, CRM software, and any referral fees paid to attorneys or third parties.

## How to Calculate Your True Cost Per Case

Start with three months of data — long enough to smooth out seasonal variation, recent enough to reflect current channel performance.

**Step 1: Total all acquisition spend**

Include:
- Paid search (Google Ads, Bing)
- Paid social (Meta, YouTube, programmatic display)
- SEO agency retainer or in-house SEO salary allocation
- Lead vendor fees (per-lead or per-case pricing)
- Referral fees where permitted by applicable bar rules
- Intake staff compensation, pro-rated to time spent on marketing-generated leads
- CRM and call tracking software costs

Do not include general overhead, malpractice insurance, or associate salaries unrelated to intake. Those are operating costs, not acquisition costs.

**Step 2: Isolate signed cases by source**

This requires a properly configured CRM with source attribution at the lead level. If your intake team isn't logging where every lead originated, you're calculating a blended average that tells you almost nothing about which channels to invest in or cut.

**Step 3: Divide and segment**

Calculate overall cost per case first, then recalculate by channel. A blended $1,200 CPC that's actually $600 from one source and $2,400 from another is not a single data point — it's a decision waiting to be made.

## Industry Benchmarks by Practice Area

Benchmarks vary significantly by case type, geography, and brand maturity. That said, broad market data provides useful anchors.

According to the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/), legal marketing spend as a percentage of revenue ranges from 2% to 18% depending on practice area and growth stage. For high-volume consumer practices, the operational pressure is on throughput and acquisition cost containment.

**Rough CPC benchmarks by practice area (2025 market):**

- **Personal injury (auto accident):** $800–$2,500 per signed case
- **Mass tort / MDL:** $1,500–$6,000+ per plaintiff, varying by litigation stage and inventory demand
- **Workers' compensation:** $400–$1,200 per case
- **Family law:** $300–$900 per matter
- **Social Security disability:** $150–$500 per signed claimant
- **Criminal defense:** $200–$700 per retained client

These ranges reflect blended costs across channels. Firms running [exclusive legal leads](/blog/exclusive-legal-leads) rather than shared lead pools typically pay a higher per-lead cost but see materially lower cost per *signed* case because the lead-to-retainer conversion rate is higher.

## The Hidden Costs That Inflate Your Case Acquisition Spend

The most common mistake is treating cost per case as a marketing metric. It's actually a cross-functional metric. Three cost categories get consistently underestimated.

**Intake labor costs**

A two-person intake team handling 500 leads per month to sign 40 cases generates zero revenue during the 460 conversations that go nowhere. That labor cost belongs in the cost-per-case calculation — fully loaded. The [Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html) has documented that intake inefficiency is one of the top contributors to law firm profitability erosion across practice types.

**Speed-to-lead decay**

Every hour between a lead submitting a form and your team making contact degrades conversion probability. Research consistently shows contact rates drop by 80% or more after the first five minutes. Slow follow-up doesn't just lose cases — it artificially inflates your cost per case by reducing the conversion rate on leads you've already paid for.

For practical ways to address this, see our post on [improving your law firm's lead conversion rate](/blog/law-firm-conversion-rate).

**Lead duplication across vendors**

If you're buying from multiple lead vendors without deduplication logic in your CRM, you're paying for the same prospect twice while crediting the wrong channel for the conversion. A mid-sized PI firm running three lead sources without cross-channel deduplication can easily see 15–25% of its legal marketing spend wasted on duplicate contacts.

## How to Reduce Your Law Firm Cost Per Case Without Cutting Quality

Cutting marketing budget to lower cost per case is a trap. You're not reducing costs — you're reducing cases. The goal is better economics per dollar, not fewer dollars spent.

**Five levers that actually move the number:**

1. **Improve intake conversion.** A 30% improvement in lead-to-retainer conversion cuts cost per case by 23% without touching the marketing budget. Invest in intake training, faster response protocols, and better screening scripts before renegotiating any vendor contracts.
2. **Shift spend toward higher-converting channels.** Not all leads are priced at their true value. If paid search leads convert at 12% and shared vendor leads convert at 4%, the "cheaper" source may not hold up under full-cost analysis.
3. **Use exclusive rather than shared leads.** The premium on exclusive over shared lead pricing is typically 40–100%. But if shared leads require three times the intake effort to produce the same number of signed cases, exclusivity often wins on a cost-per-case basis.
4. **Build source attribution.** You cannot optimize what you cannot see. Every marketing dollar that flows into an untracked source is a dollar you cannot reallocate intelligently next quarter.
5. **Align intake capacity to lead volume.** Bottlenecked intake is the hidden multiplier on cost per case. If your team is too small to work leads within the first hour, you're degrading the economics of every channel simultaneously.

The [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/) provides guidance on fee-sharing and referral structures that can also affect true acquisition cost depending on your sourcing mix — worth reviewing before structuring any third-party lead arrangements.

## Measuring ROI Beyond Cost Per Case

Cost per case is an input metric. The output metric that matters is **return on acquisition spend** — revenue generated per dollar of marketing investment, adjusted for case lifecycle.

A personal injury firm with a $1,500 cost per case and an average contingency recovery of $18,000 has a 12x return on acquisition spend. The same firm paying $2,500 per case on a channel that consistently produces $30,000 average recoveries also has a 12x return — on a better-quality pipeline. The CPC is higher. The economics are identical.

This is why cost per case must always be evaluated alongside:

- **Average fee per case** (or expected recovery on contingency matters)
- **Case close rate** (the percentage of signed cases that reach resolution)
- **Time to resolution** (cash flow timing matters for firm operations)
- **Client lifetime value** (referrals, repeat matters, cross-practice opportunities)

## Building a Case Acquisition Strategy That Compounds

Optimizing law firm cost per case is a quarterly discipline, not a one-time audit. Channels shift, competition intensifies, and lead quality degrades over time without active management.

Firms that sustain the best acquisition economics over multi-year periods tend to share a few operating characteristics: they track cost per case by channel in real time, they hold vendors accountable to conversion metrics not just delivery volume, and they treat intake performance as a marketing lever — not just an operational function.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good cost per case for a personal injury law firm?

A competitive cost per case for personal injury firms ranges from $800 to $2,500, depending on case type, geography, and lead channel. Firms using exclusive leads and well-optimized intake processes typically land in the lower half of that range. The more useful benchmark is cost per case relative to average case value — aim for a ratio of at least 8:1 on contingency matters.

### How do I calculate my law firm's cost per case?

Divide total acquisition spend — including paid media, lead vendor fees, intake labor, and technology costs — by the number of signed retainers in the same period. Use only marketing-attributed spend and cases, and segment by channel to identify which sources are performing above or below your target ratio.

### Why is my law firm cost per case higher than industry benchmarks?

The most common causes are low intake conversion rates, slow lead response times, lead duplication across vendors, and incomplete source attribution. Before assuming the problem is channel or lead quality, audit how quickly your intake team responds to new leads and what percentage of contacted leads are converting to signed retainers.

### Does using exclusive leads lower cost per case?

In most cases, yes. Exclusive leads cost more per unit than shared leads but typically convert at two to four times the rate, meaning fewer leads are needed to sign the same number of cases. When fully loaded with intake labor and blended conversion rates, exclusive leads often produce a lower cost per signed case despite the higher unit price. See our breakdown of [exclusive vs. shared legal leads](/blog/exclusive-legal-leads) for a detailed comparison.

### How often should I review my cost per case by channel?

Monthly at minimum, with a deeper quarterly review that incorporates case value data. Monthly reviews catch deteriorating channel performance before it compounds. Quarterly reviews let you evaluate cost per case against downstream metrics like average fee per matter, which often aren't available until 60–90 days after signing.
