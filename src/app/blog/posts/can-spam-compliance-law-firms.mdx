Most attorneys assume email is the safe channel. No dialer, no carrier filters, no consent-capture debates, just a newsletter and a send button. That assumption is exactly why **CAN-SPAM compliance for law firms** gets ignored until a complaint lands.

The CAN-SPAM Act is enforced by the FTC, and civil penalties exceed **$50,000 per violating email**. A firm that blasts a list of 5,000 prospects with a missing opt-out link has not made a small mistake. It has created 5,000 separate violations.

## What CAN-SPAM Actually Covers for Law Firms

CAN-SPAM applies to any email whose primary purpose is to advertise or promote a commercial product or service. Legal services qualify. A "Free case evaluation" email, a practice-area promotion, or a seminar invitation is a commercial message.

The law does not distinguish between B2C and B2B email, and it does not require the recipient to be a stranger. Sending to your own past prospects is still regulated.

### Commercial vs. transactional vs. relationship messages

The FTC sorts email into three buckets:

- **Commercial:** promotional content. Fully subject to CAN-SPAM.
- **Transactional or relationship:** engagement letters, billing notices, case status updates, and other messages tied to an existing client matter. These are exempt from most rules, but they still cannot contain false or misleading routing information.
- **Mixed:** a case update with a promotional block bolted on. The FTC looks at the subject line and the first lines of the body to decide the primary purpose.

The mixed category is where firms get burned. A "client newsletter" that is mostly referral requests and new service pitches is a commercial email, regardless of what you call it.

## The Seven Requirements Every Commercial Email Must Meet

The statute is short, and most violations come from skipping basic mechanics. Every commercial message needs:

1. **Accurate header information.** The From, To, and routing fields must identify the real sender. No spoofed domains, no misleading display names.
2. **Non-deceptive subject lines.** "Re: your accident claim" sent to someone who never contacted you is a deceptive subject line.
3. **Clear identification as an advertisement.** The format can be flexible, but the recipient must be able to tell it is a solicitation.
4. **A valid physical postal address.** A street address, a registered P.O. box, or a private mailbox all qualify.
5. **A visible, working opt-out mechanism.** A one-click link or a reply-to-unsubscribe process.
6. **Opt-outs honored within 10 business days.** The opt-out must function for at least 30 days after the message is sent, and you cannot charge a fee or require more than an email reply or a single web page visit.
7. **Monitoring of anyone sending on your behalf.** This is the one most firms miss.

## Why Vendors Make CAN-SPAM Compliance for Law Firms Harder

Under CAN-SPAM, both the company whose product is advertised and the company that sends the message can be held liable. Hiring an agency or lead vendor does not transfer your exposure.

If a marketing partner emails scraped addresses on your behalf with your firm name in the body, your firm is a legally responsible party. The same logic applies to co-registration lists, purchased email databases, and "warm" lists from third parties.

This is why the due diligence in our [data broker compliance checklist](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) belongs in every vendor contract. Ask where addresses came from, how opt-outs are synchronized, and who answers when the FTC asks.

### Contract terms worth requiring

- A warranty that all addresses were collected without scraping or harvesting.
- A requirement to honor your suppression list within 10 business days, ideally sooner.
- Indemnification for violations caused by the vendor's sending practices.
- The right to audit sending records on request.

## Bar Rules Add a Second Layer on Top of the Federal Law

Passing the FTC test is not enough. Attorney email is also advertising and solicitation under state bar rules, which differ from the federal standard in important ways.

ABA [Model Rule 7.3](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/) restricts live person-to-person solicitation, but written and electronic communications are generally permitted if they are truthful and not misleading. Many states go further and require labels such as "Advertising Material," filing requirements for certain solicitation letters, or specific disclaimers.

Our overview of [attorney advertising compliance](/blog/attorney-advertising-compliance) covers the label and disclosure patterns by jurisdiction. Build both sets of rules into one email template so compliance is automatic, not a case-by-case judgment call.

## Building a Compliance Workflow That Scales

Good policy has to survive a busy Tuesday. These controls keep most firms out of trouble.

### Centralize suppression

Maintain **one master suppression list** that every platform checks before sending: your email service provider, your CRM, and any agency tools. An unsubscribe in one system that never reaches the others is the classic cause of repeat violations.

### Standardize your template

Lock the footer. The postal address, opt-out link, and bar-required disclaimer should be hard-coded into the template, not typed by whoever is sending that day.

### Audit quarterly

Pull a sample of 20 recent sends and check each against the seven requirements. Test the unsubscribe flow yourself. Confirm it takes effect and that the address stops receiving messages.

## Email Is Not Exempt From Other Rules

CAN-SPAM sets a floor for email, not a ceiling. If your campaign also uses text messages, you are in TCPA territory, where consent standards are far stricter. State privacy statutes may also govern how you collect and use email addresses in the first place. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does CAN-SPAM apply to law firms?

Yes, CAN-SPAM applies to law firms whenever an email's primary purpose is to promote legal services. It does not matter whether the recipient is a consumer or a business. Purely transactional client communications, such as billing notices and case updates, are largely exempt.

### What is the penalty for violating CAN-SPAM compliance as a law firm?

The FTC can seek civil penalties of more than $50,000 for each separate violating email. Penalties are assessed per message, so a single flawed campaign can multiply quickly. Knowingly sending deceptive messages can also lead to criminal charges in aggravated cases.

### How long does a law firm have to honor an unsubscribe request?

A law firm must honor an opt-out request within 10 business days. The unsubscribe mechanism must remain functional for at least 30 days after the email is sent. You cannot charge a fee, require a login, or ask for more information than an email address.

### Can a law firm email people who never contacted them?

Yes, CAN-SPAM does not require prior consent, but all other requirements still apply, and state bar advertising rules may impose additional limits. Opt-in is not required under federal law, which is different from the consent standard for texts and calls. Using a purchased list adds vendor liability risk.

### Is a law firm newsletter considered a commercial email?

A newsletter is commercial if its primary purpose is promoting your services. Mostly educational content with a brief footer may qualify as non-commercial, but the FTC looks at the overall message. When in doubt, include the opt-out and postal address anyway.
