A lead fills out a form, your intake team calls and texts within minutes, and the case signs. Six months later, a demand letter arrives claiming the person never agreed to be contacted. The only question that matters is whether your **lead consent records** can answer it.

Most firms cannot. They have a name, a phone number, and a source label in the CRM, but no timestamp, no consent language, and no way to show what the person actually saw. When a dispute starts, the burden usually falls on the party that made the contact, and that party is you.

## Why Law Firm Lead Consent Records Matter More Than Lead Quality

Consent is not a marketing detail. It is the legal basis for every call, text, and email your intake team sends, and it travels with the lead into your systems.

The [Telephone Consumer Protection Act](/blog/tcpa-compliance-law-firms) allows statutory damages of $500 per violation, rising to $1,500 for willful ones. A single bad lead list contacted by call and text can turn into a class action quickly, which is why TCPA filings have remained a steady fixture of consumer litigation.

The firm that placed the contact is a typical defendant, regardless of who generated the lead. "Our vendor said the leads were opted in" is not a defense. It is a claim you must be able to document.

## What a Defensible Consent Record Contains

A usable record answers who, what, when, and how. If any element is missing, the record is weak.

Capture these fields for every lead:

- **Identity:** name, phone number, and email exactly as submitted
- **Timestamp:** date and time of consent, with time zone
- **Consent language:** the exact disclosure text shown beside the submit button, including version
- **Method:** web form, call, text keyword, or in-person, plus the page URL where it happened
- **Technical evidence:** IP address, device or browser data, and a session identifier
- **Source:** the vendor, campaign, and the sellers named in the disclosure

The disclosure text is the piece firms most often lose. Forms get redesigned, copy changes, and nobody archived what the old version said. Version your consent language and store every revision with its effective dates.

### Screenshots Are Not Enough

A screenshot of a form proves what a page looked like on a day. It does not prove that a specific person saw it and submitted it. Pair the language archive with a session-level record that links the submission to the exact disclosure shown.

## The One-to-One Consent Rule Is Gone, but the Burden Is Not

In 2023 the FCC adopted a rule requiring consent to be given to one seller at a time, which would have ended the practice of a single checkbox covering dozens of "partners." The Eleventh Circuit vacated that rule in January 2025 in a case brought by the Insurance Marketing Coalition, before it took effect.

Some firms read that as relief. It is narrower than it sounds. Consent still has to be prior, express, and tied to the type of contact. Courts and state laws, including mini-TCPA statutes in states such as Florida and Oklahoma, can still scrutinize vague, bundled consent.

The safer reading: treat one-to-one consent as best practice even though it is no longer mandated. A lead that names your firm specifically is far easier to defend than one that names a list of unnamed affiliates. Coverage in the [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com) tracks how courts continue to treat bundled consent.

## Building a Consent Record System Your Intake Team Will Actually Use

Compliance fails when it depends on memory. The goal is a system where the record attaches automatically.

1. **Store consent at the lead level.** Add consent timestamp, language version, and source fields to your [legal CRM](/blog/legal-crm-for-law-firms) so they are never separate from the contact.
2. **Block outreach without consent.** Configure the CRM so texts and automated calls cannot fire when the consent field is empty.
3. **Log revocations immediately.** Opt-outs must be honored promptly, and a "stop" sent by text, email, or call all count. Sync them across every channel your team uses.
4. **Record your own calls.** Consent captured by phone needs a recording or note, and recording itself has legal requirements, as covered in our guide to [call recording compliance](/blog/law-firm-call-recording-compliance).
5. **Cross-check suppression lists.** Compare leads against the [Do Not Call registry](/blog/law-firm-do-not-call-compliance) and your internal opt-out list before outreach.

The test is simple: pick a lead at random and see how long it takes to produce the full record. If it takes more than a few minutes, the system is not working.

## How Long to Keep Law Firm Lead Consent Records

Keep records at least as long as the longest limitations period that could apply. The TCPA's practical limitations period is four years under the federal catch-all statute, and some state claims run longer.

Many compliance teams set a retention window of five years from the last contact, then review. Longer retention conflicts with data minimization principles under state privacy laws, so set a defined schedule rather than keeping everything forever. Our post on [data retention policy](/blog/legal-data-retention-policy) walks through how to balance both pressures.

Do not delete consent evidence for a lead involved in a complaint or claim. Litigation holds override your normal schedule.

## What to Demand From Lead Vendors

Your vendor's records are only as good as the contract that obligates them to share. Before buying, run the checks in our [data broker compliance](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) checklist, then add these terms:

- Delivery of consent evidence with every lead, not on request after a dispute
- The exact disclosure language and version for each source
- A warranty that consent names your firm, with indemnification if it does not
- Audit rights and prompt production of records within a fixed number of days
- Clear refund and replacement terms for leads with defective consent, as discussed in our piece on [legal lead refund policy](/blog/legal-lead-refund-policy)

A vendor that cannot hand over a consent record at the moment of delivery is asking you to carry their risk. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should law firm lead consent records include?

Law firm lead consent records should include the person's identity, a timestamp, the exact consent language shown, the method of consent, and technical evidence such as IP address and session ID. They should also name the source and any sellers listed in the disclosure. Without all of these, consent is difficult to prove in a dispute.

### How long should a law firm keep TCPA consent records?

A law firm should keep TCPA consent records for at least four years, and many compliance teams use five years from the last contact. Some state claims have longer periods. Records tied to a complaint or lawsuit must be preserved until the matter ends.

### Is one-to-one consent still required for purchased leads?

No, the FCC's one-to-one consent rule was vacated by the Eleventh Circuit in January 2025 before it took effect. Consent must still be prior, express, and specific to the contact method. Leads that name your firm directly remain the most defensible.

### Who is liable if a lead vendor fabricates consent?

The firm that contacted the lead is commonly named as a defendant, even if the vendor generated the false consent. A vendor indemnification clause may let you recover losses, but it does not stop the claim from being filed against you. That is why firms should verify records before outreach, not after a complaint.
