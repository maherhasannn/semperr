Every lead vendor has a back catalog. Inquiries that did not sell in real time, or sold once and were never signed, get repackaged weeks later as **aged legal leads** and offered at a steep discount. The pitch is hard to ignore: the same consumer, the same legal problem, a fraction of the price.

The discount is real. So is the reason for it. An old inquiry has usually been called by other firms, may already be represented, and comes with a consent record that has been getting weaker every day since the form was submitted.

That does not make aged legal leads worthless. It makes it a different product with different math, and firms that treat it like discounted fresh inventory are the ones who lose money on it.

## What Aged Legal Leads Actually Are

There is no industry standard for the term. Most sellers call a lead "aged" once it is more than 30 days old, but some start the clock at 72 hours and others sell files that are a year old or more. The first question for any seller is the exact submission date on every record, not an age bracket.

Aged legal leads generally come from three places:

- **Unsold real-time leads**: inquiries that arrived outside a buyer's hours, geography, or daily cap and never found a home.
- **Sold-but-unsigned leads**: inquiries that were delivered to one or more firms and did not convert, then resold after an exclusivity window expired.
- **Recycled or aggregated files**: records that have passed through several hands, often with no reliable history of who contacted the consumer or what they were told.

The first category is the only one that resembles fresh demand that simply aged on a shelf. The second has already been filtered by someone else's intake team, which means the easiest cases are gone. The third is where most of the compliance trouble lives.

If a seller cannot tell you which category a file belongs to, assume the third.

## Why Aged Legal Leads Are So Cheap

Lead value decays fast, and the reason is behavioral. A person with a legal problem contacts more than one firm and tends to hire whoever has a real conversation with them first. We covered that dynamic in detail in our piece on [speed to lead for law firms](/blog/speed-to-lead-law-firms).

By the time a lead is 60 days old, one of four things has usually happened:

1. The consumer hired another firm.
2. The consumer resolved the problem without a lawyer, or gave up on it.
3. The consumer's contact details changed or were never accurate.
4. The consumer still has the problem and nobody followed up properly.

Only the fourth group is worth anything, and **aged lead pricing** reflects how small that group is. You are not buying a lead at a discount. You are buying a list where most of the records are dead, and paying a low price per record to find the few that are not.

That fourth group does exist, though. The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has run secret-shopper studies for years and consistently finds that a large share of firms never answer calls or reply to inquiries at all. Some consumers sitting in an aged file were simply never served by the firm that bought them first.

## Where Aged Leads Convert and Where They Do Not

The single best predictor of whether an old inquiry is still live is how long the consumer's decision takes in that practice area. Urgent matters age badly. Slow, deliberated matters age well.

### Practice areas that age badly

**Aged personal injury leads** in single-event categories like car accidents are the weakest version of the product. The injured person is contacted by multiple firms within days, and anyone with a viable claim is typically signed within the first couple of weeks. What remains at 90 days skews toward minor injuries, disputed liability, and people who were turned down elsewhere.

Criminal defense and DUI are worse. The arraignment has passed and counsel is already in place. There is almost no scenario where a 60-day-old criminal inquiry is still an open opportunity.

### Practice areas that age well

Other matters have decision cycles measured in months, and the consumer's need does not expire on a short clock:

- **Mass torts**: a claimant who used a product or was exposed to a contaminant still qualifies months later, and many never completed a retainer the first time.
- **Bankruptcy and debt relief**: people often inquire long before they are ready to file. Financial pressure tends to build, not resolve.
- **Social Security disability**: an initial denial or a worsening condition can turn an old inquiry into a motivated client.
- **Estate planning and family law**: procrastination is the norm. A six-month-old inquiry frequently describes a decision that still has not been made.
- **Immigration**: timelines are long and circumstances change, which reopens conversations that went quiet.

In these categories, aged leads for law firms can function less like a discount bin and more like a low-cost re-engagement list. That only holds if the statute of limitations and eligibility criteria still work, which your team needs to check on the first call.

## The Real Cost: Cost Per Signed Case, Not Cost Per Lead

A cheap lead can still produce an expensive case. The only number that matters is what you spend, including labor, to get one retainer signed.

Here is an illustrative comparison. The figures are hypothetical and exist only to show the structure of the math.

| | Fresh exclusive lead | Aged lead |
|---|---|---|
| Price per lead | $250 | $15 |
| Leads purchased | 40 | 1,000 |
| Lead spend | $10,000 | $15,000 |
| Signed cases (10% vs. 0.6%) | 4 | 6 |
| Intake labor hours | 20 | 170 |
| Labor cost at $30/hour | $600 | $5,100 |
| **Total cost per signed case** | **$2,650** | **$3,350** |

In this example the aged file produces more cases and still loses on cost per case, because the labor required to work a thousand cold records swamps the savings on price. Change the conversion rate from 0.6% to 1.2% and the aged file wins comfortably.

That sensitivity is the whole point. With aged legal leads, a fraction of a percentage point in conversion decides whether the purchase was smart or wasteful, and you will not know your rate until you test.

Three rules follow from the math:

- **Count labor honestly.** Ten dial attempts per record across a thousand records is a full-time job. If that work lands on the same people who answer your live calls, your best leads suffer.
- **Automate the first touches.** Aged files only pencil out when texts, emails, and voicemail drops do the sorting and humans speak only to people who respond. A structured [lead nurturing sequence](/blog/law-firm-lead-nurturing) is a prerequisite, not an add-on.
- **Test small.** Buy a few hundred records in one practice area, track contact rate and signed cases for 60 days, and scale only if cost per case beats your other channels.

## The Compliance Problems That Come With Buying Aged Leads

Price is the visible risk with aged legal leads. Consent is the expensive one. A Telephone Consumer Protection Act claim carries statutory damages of $500 per call or text, rising to $1,500 for willful violations, and aged files are where those claims tend to originate.

### Consent gets weaker with time

Four specific problems show up when buying aged leads:

1. **Consent may not cover your firm.** The consumer agreed to be contacted by whoever was named on the original form. If your firm was not identified, you need to understand what that consent actually authorized before you send an automated text.
2. **Do Not Call exemptions expire.** Under federal rules, a consumer inquiry creates an established business relationship that permits calls to a number on the National Do Not Call Registry for only three months. A 120-day-old lead has lost that exemption, and the relationship was arguably the seller's, not yours.
3. **Phone numbers get reassigned.** The person who answers a year-old number may not be the person who filled out the form. The FCC maintains a Reassigned Numbers Database for exactly this reason, and checking it provides a safe harbor.
4. **Opt-outs may not have traveled with the file.** A consumer who told the first buyer to stop calling has revoked consent. If the seller did not suppress that record, the next call is yours to answer for.

Before any outreach, get the original consent language, timestamp, and source URL for every record, and scrub the file against the registry and your own internal list. Our guides to [lead consent records](/blog/law-firm-lead-consent-records) and [Do Not Call compliance](/blog/law-firm-do-not-call-compliance) walk through what that documentation should look like.

### Bar rules still apply

A consumer who asked to hear from a lawyer has invited contact, which is why responding to a fresh inquiry is not treated as prohibited solicitation. That invitation becomes harder to rely on when the request went to a different company months ago. [ABA Model Rule 7.3](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_3_solicitation_of_clients/) restricts live person-to-person solicitation, and state versions vary enough that you should confirm your own jurisdiction's position before dialing old files.

Representation is the other trap. Many people in an aged file have already hired counsel. Your script needs to ask about existing representation in the first thirty seconds and end the pitch when the answer is yes.

Information the consumer shared on the original form also deserves care. The duties owed to prospective clients under [Model Rule 1.18](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_18_duties_of_prospective_client/) attach to people who consult a lawyer about forming a relationship, and a file full of case details should be handled accordingly.

## How to Evaluate an Aged Lead Seller

Most of the risk can be screened out before you pay. Ask these questions and treat a vague answer as a no:

- What is the exact submission date and time for each record?
- Where did the lead originate, and can you see the form and consent language the consumer saw?
- How many times has each record been sold, and to what types of buyers?
- Was the lead ever contacted, and are opt-outs and litigant numbers suppressed?
- Is the file exclusive to you from this point forward, or will it be sold again next month?
- What is the replacement policy for disconnected numbers and already-represented consumers?

The resale question matters more than most buyers realize. The same file sold to five firms produces five rounds of calls to the same tired consumers, which is the worst version of the [shared lead problem](/blog/exclusive-legal-leads).

A seller with clean inventory can answer all six questions from their own system. A seller who cannot is brokering someone else's file and does not know what is in it.

## The Best Aged Leads Are the Ones You Already Own

Before buying anyone else's old data, look at your own. Every firm has a CRM full of inquiries that were never reached, consultations that never booked, and prospects who said "not yet."

Those records beat any purchased file on every dimension. The consumer contacted your firm by name, the consent is yours, the history is known, and the lead costs nothing. A quarterly reactivation campaign to your own unconverted inquiries will usually outperform purchased old legal leads at the same age.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are aged legal leads?

Aged legal leads are consumer inquiries for legal services that are resold after the original real-time delivery window has passed, typically 30 days or more after submission. They come from unsold inventory, leads that another firm bought but did not sign, or recycled data files. They cost far less than fresh leads because most of the consumers have already hired counsel, resolved the issue, or become unreachable.

### Are aged leads for law firms worth buying?

Aged leads can be worth buying in practice areas with long decision cycles, such as mass torts, bankruptcy, disability, and estate planning. They rarely pay off in urgent categories like car accidents or criminal defense, where the consumer hires within days. The deciding factor is total cost per signed case, including the intake labor needed to work a large, mostly unresponsive list.

### How much do aged legal leads cost?

Aged lead pricing is usually a small fraction of the fresh price for the same practice area, and it falls further as the data gets older. Price depends on age, practice area, how many times the record has been sold, and whether the file is exclusive. A low price per record says nothing about value until you measure contact rate and signed cases on a test batch.

### Is it legal to call or text aged legal leads?

It can be, but the consent attached to an old inquiry is weaker than most buyers assume. You need proof that the consumer's original consent covers your firm, a Do Not Call scrub, a check for reassigned numbers, and confirmation that prior opt-outs were suppressed. State bar solicitation rules also apply, so confirm your jurisdiction's position before contacting old files.

### How old is too old for aged personal injury leads?

For single-event injury cases, most of the value is gone within the first few weeks, because viable claimants are signed quickly. Beyond 90 days, the remaining pool skews toward weak claims and represented parties, and the statute of limitations becomes a live issue on older files. Mass tort inquiries are the exception and can remain viable for many months.
