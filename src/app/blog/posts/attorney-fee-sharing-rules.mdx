Most managing partners assume that if a vendor sells them "leads" instead of "referrals," they're automatically in the clear. They're not. **Attorney fee-sharing rules** don't care what a contract calls the transaction — they care about how the money moves and what the payment is actually for. A firm that structures a lead-buying arrangement the wrong way can violate the same rule that bars splitting a fee with a non-lawyer, even if nobody involved intended to break it.

This matters more now than it did five years ago. Legal lead generation has become a real industry, with dozens of vendors selling everything from shared internet leads to exclusive, pre-qualified intake. The firms that scale case volume without ethics exposure are the ones that understand exactly where the fee-sharing line sits — and structure every vendor contract to stay on the right side of it.

## Attorney Fee-Sharing Rules Start With ABA Model Rule 5.4

Rule 5.4 of the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct is the foundation of every state's fee-sharing restriction. It prohibits a lawyer or law firm from sharing legal fees with a non-lawyer, with narrow exceptions for things like retirement plans and estate payments to a deceased lawyer's heirs.

The rule exists to protect **professional independence**. Regulators worry that if a non-lawyer's compensation depends on the outcome or size of a legal fee, that non-lawyer gains influence over how the case is handled — who gets referred, how it's staffed, whether it settles early. That's the harm the rule targets, and it's why enforcement focuses on *how* a payment is calculated, not what it's labeled.

A few structures reliably trigger scrutiny under attorney fee-sharing rules:

- **Percentage-of-fee arrangements** — paying a vendor a cut of the eventual settlement or verdict
- **Contingent payment on case outcome** — any fee tied to whether the case resolves favorably
- **Ongoing revenue splits** — recurring payments structured to mirror a partnership interest
- **Disguised co-counsel arrangements** — treating a non-lawyer marketing vendor as if they were referring counsel entitled to a fee split

You can read the full text of the rule directly from the [ABA's Model Rule 5.4](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_5_4_professional_independence_of_a_lawyer/) page, which most state bars use as the template for their own version.

## What ABA Rule 7.2 Actually Permits

Rule 7.2 is the provision that makes lead generation possible at all. It permits lawyers to pay the "reasonable costs" of advertising and to pay for a "qualified lawyer referral service," provided the payment isn't structured as a percentage of the fee earned. This is the distinction that separates a compliant pay-per-lead vendor from an illegal referral-fee scheme.

Under [ABA Model Rule 7.2](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_communications_concerning_a_lawyers_services_specific_rules/), a firm can generally pay:

- A **flat fee per lead**, regardless of whether the case converts or settles
- A **flat fee per case signed**, as long as it isn't tied to the eventual fee amount or outcome
- Standard **advertising costs**, including digital media spend, SEO services, and content production
- Fees to a **bar-approved or bar-operated lawyer referral service** that meets state certification requirements

What it does not permit is any arrangement where the vendor's payment rises or falls with the size of the settlement, the contingency fee percentage, or the case outcome. That's the test every contract should be run through before signing.

## Pay-Per-Lead vs. Pay-Per-Referral: Why the Distinction Matters

The terminology vendors use often blurs a distinction that matters enormously under attorney fee-sharing rules. A **lead** is contact information and interest — a prospective client who has expressed intent and meets basic qualification criteria. A **referral**, in the traditional sense the rules were written around, implies an ongoing relationship where the referring party expects compensation tied to the outcome.

Buying leads at a flat, pre-set price is advertising spend. Paying a "referral fee" that scales with the size of the case is fee-splitting, and it's prohibited in the overwhelming majority of jurisdictions regardless of who the non-lawyer party is.

This is exactly why the structure of a lead vendor's pricing model matters as much as its lead quality. A firm evaluating vendors should be asking the same questions covered in [how to vet legal lead generation companies](/blog/legal-lead-generation-companies) — but with particular attention to whether pricing is flat-fee or outcome-linked. If a vendor's pitch includes any version of "we get a piece when you settle," that's a rule 5.4 problem regardless of how the contract is worded.

## State Bar Variations: Where the Rules Get Stricter

States adopt their own versions of Model Rules 5.4 and 7.2, and several deviate meaningfully from the ABA template:

1. **New Jersey and Ohio** maintain some of the strictest interpretations, with limited tolerance for pay-per-lead arrangements that resemble referral fees even at flat rates.
2. **Texas** requires specific disclosures when a lawyer pays for advertising through a lead generation service, distinct from paying a referral service.
3. **Florida** has detailed rules distinguishing "lawyer referral services" (which must be bar-qualified) from general advertising, with real consequences for miscategorizing a vendor relationship.
4. **California**, following its 2023 fee-sharing reforms, now permits limited nonlawyer ownership structures in specific pilot contexts — but general fee-splitting prohibitions still apply to standard practice.

Firms operating in multiple states need to check the applicable version of the rule in every jurisdiction where they're buying leads, not just where the firm is headquartered. A structure that's compliant in one state can be a violation in another.

## How to Structure Lead Generation Spend to Stay Compliant

The safest way to buy leads without triggering attorney fee-sharing rules is to keep every payment structurally identical to a media buy. Practical guardrails:

- **Insist on flat, pre-set pricing** — per lead or per signed case, never a percentage of fee or settlement
- **Get it in writing** that the vendor's compensation doesn't vary based on case outcome, size, or duration
- **Verify the vendor isn't a "referral service"** requiring bar certification in your state — if it is, confirm they're actually registered
- **Keep records of consent and sourcing**, which also protects the firm on the [data broker compliance](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) side of the transaction
- **Review vendor contracts annually**, since pricing models change as vendors add new products

This overlaps directly with how firms should already be thinking about [attorney advertising compliance](/blog/attorney-advertising-compliance) more broadly — fee-sharing risk and advertising risk usually show up in the same vendor relationships, so it makes sense to review both at once.

## Red Flags That Signal a Fee-Sharing Violation

A handful of patterns should stop a deal before signature:

- The vendor asks for a **percentage of the settlement or verdict** instead of a flat fee
- Pricing is described as **"success-based"** or **"performance-based"** in a way tied to case outcome rather than lead delivery or qualification
- The vendor calls itself a **"referral partner"** but isn't registered as a lawyer referral service in your state
- Contract language leaves **pricing open-ended**, to be determined after the case resolves
- The arrangement requires the firm to give the vendor **ongoing information about case status or settlement value**

Any one of these should trigger a compliance review before the contract is signed, not after a bar complaint arrives.

## Building a Compliant Lead Acquisition Program

Firms that scale case volume responsibly treat fee-sharing compliance as a procurement standard, not a one-off legal question. That means every new vendor gets evaluated against the same checklist: flat pricing, no outcome linkage, proper referral-service registration where applicable, and clean documentation of how leads are sourced and consented.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a law firm pay a percentage of the fee for a referral?

No. Under ABA Model Rule 5.4 and nearly every state's equivalent, a law firm cannot share legal fees with a non-lawyer, including paying a percentage of a settlement or contingency fee to a lead vendor or referral source. Payments must be flat and unrelated to case outcome to stay compliant.

### What's the difference between paying for leads and paying for referrals?

Paying for leads means paying a flat, pre-set price for contact information and qualified interest, which is treated as advertising spend under Rule 7.2. Paying for referrals traditionally implies compensation tied to the outcome or size of the case, which falls under the fee-sharing restrictions of Rule 5.4.

### Do attorney fee-sharing rules apply to marketing agencies too?

Yes. Any non-lawyer entity — including marketing agencies, lead generation companies, and referral platforms — is subject to the same fee-sharing restrictions if payment is structured as a percentage of legal fees. The rule applies based on how compensation is calculated, not the vendor's job title.

### Is it legal to buy exclusive leads for a law firm?

Yes, buying exclusive leads at a flat, pre-set price is legal and complies with attorney fee-sharing rules, as long as the payment doesn't vary based on case outcome or settlement value. Exclusivity refers to lead delivery, not fee structure, and the two are independent compliance questions.

### What happens if a law firm violates fee-sharing rules?

Violations can result in state bar discipline ranging from a reprimand to suspension or disbarment, depending on severity and jurisdiction. Firms found in violation may also face unenforceability of related contracts and reputational damage that affects future vendor and referral relationships.
