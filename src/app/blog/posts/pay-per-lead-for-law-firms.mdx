A $75 lead sounds cheap until you realize it took 40 of them to sign one case. Then the real price is $3,000 per retainer, before you count the intake hours spent on the other 39. That gap between sticker price and signed-case price is the central problem with **pay per lead for law firms**.

The model isn't broken. It's just misunderstood, and most firms buy it based on the per-lead number instead of the number that actually determines profit.

## How Pay Per Lead for Law Firms Actually Works

Under a pay per lead arrangement, you pay a fixed fee every time a vendor delivers a contact that meets agreed criteria. The vendor owns the marketing risk. You own the conversion risk.

That split is the whole story. Once a lead is delivered, the vendor is paid whether it signs, ghosts, or turns out to be a wrong number.

Typical pricing varies widely by practice area and case value:

- **Family law and general practice:** often $30 to $120 per lead
- **Personal injury:** commonly $100 to $400 per lead, depending on injury severity and geography
- **Mass tort and med mal:** frequently $300 to $1,500+ per qualified lead, because qualification requirements are strict
- **Workers' compensation and employment:** usually in the $75 to $300 range

These ranges are directional, not guarantees. Vendors price to what the market will bear, and competitive markets like Los Angeles or Houston run well above the national average.

## The Math That Matters: Cost Per Lead vs. Cost Per Signed Case

The only metric that matters is what you spend to sign one client. Cost per lead is an input, not an outcome.

Here is the formula:

1. Take your price per lead.
2. Divide by your lead-to-signed conversion rate.
3. Compare the result to the fee you expect to earn on that case type.

A $150 personal injury lead converting at 10% costs you $1,500 per signed case. The same lead converting at 4% costs $3,750. Same vendor, same invoice, completely different economics.

This is why your intake performance and your pay per lead economics are inseparable. If you haven't already, work through the framework in our guide to [law firm cost per case](/blog/law-firm-cost-per-case) before signing any volume commitment.

## What a Pay Per Lead Vendor Won't Volunteer

Pay per lead vendors sell on volume and price. The details that determine profitability sit in the contract, and they rarely come up in the sales call.

### Shared vs. exclusive delivery

A shared lead goes to three, four, or five firms at once. The first firm to reach the prospect usually wins, which means you are paying to enter a race. We break down why this matters in [exclusive vs. shared legal leads](/blog/exclusive-legal-leads).

### Return and replacement policies

Ask what counts as an invalid lead. Disconnected numbers and duplicates are usually replaced. Unqualified cases, prospects who already retained counsel, and people who never requested contact are often not.

### Lead sourcing and consent

Where did the lead come from? A form fill on a branded landing page is very different from a purchased list or a re-sold record. Consent documentation matters legally, not just commercially. See our [TCPA compliance guide for law firms](/blog/tcpa-compliance-law-firms).

## When Pay Per Lead Is the Right Model

The model earns its place under specific conditions. It works best when:

- You have a fast, disciplined intake process that reaches leads within minutes
- You can track lead-to-signed conversion by source
- Your case value comfortably absorbs a realistic cost per signed case
- You need volume flexibility without a retainer or long contract
- You are testing a new practice area or geography before committing larger budget

It works poorly when your intake is slow. The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has consistently shown that prospects contact multiple firms and often hire whoever responds first. If you take four hours to call back, you are paying for leads your competitors are signing.

## Pay Per Lead vs. Pay Per Case vs. Retainer Models

Pay per lead is one of three common structures, and each allocates risk differently.

**Pay per lead** puts conversion risk on you. It is transparent and flexible, but unforgiving of weak intake.

**Pay per signed case** puts more risk on the vendor. Per-case prices are higher, often $1,000 to $10,000+ depending on the matter, and vendors typically screen harder because they only get paid on results. Volume can be lower.

**Monthly retainer agencies** charge for effort, not outcomes. You may get strong long-term assets like SEO and content, but there is no guarantee of cases in any given month. Our guide to [choosing a law firm marketing agency](/blog/law-firm-marketing-agency) covers how to evaluate that tradeoff.

Many mature firms run a blend: retainer-based brand and SEO work for compounding returns, plus pay per lead or pay per case for predictable near-term volume.

## How to Run a Pay Per Lead Pilot

Never commit meaningful budget without a controlled test. A disciplined pilot takes 30 to 60 days and answers one question: what does a signed case from this vendor actually cost?

1. **Start small.** Buy 50 to 100 leads, not 1,000.
2. **Tag every lead by source** in your CRM so results are not blended with other channels.
3. **Measure contact rate, qualification rate, and signed rate** separately. A low contact rate points to lead quality or speed; a low qualification rate points to targeting.
4. **Audit a sample** of leads by calling them yourself and confirming they requested contact.
5. **Calculate cost per signed case** and compare it to your other channels, using the approach in [law firm marketing attribution](/blog/law-firm-marketing-attribution).

Set a kill threshold in advance. If cost per signed case exceeds a defined percentage of expected fee, you stop. Deciding after the money is spent invites rationalization.

## What Good Looks Like

Strong pay per lead programs tend to share a few traits. The vendor provides transparent sourcing, a clear replacement policy, exclusive delivery, and real-time delivery by phone or CRM integration.

On the firm side, they share intake discipline: a defined script, a response target measured in minutes, and weekly review of conversion by source. The [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/) also publishes ethics guidance on lawyer advertising and referral arrangements. Confirm any pay-per-lead arrangement fits your state's fee-sharing and solicitation rules before you begin.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does pay per lead cost for a law firm?

Pay per lead pricing for law firms typically ranges from $30 to over $1,500 per lead, depending on practice area. Family law sits at the low end, personal injury in the middle, and mass tort or medical malpractice at the top. Your cost per signed case is what counts, and it depends on your conversion rate.

### Is pay per lead worth it for personal injury lawyers?

Pay per lead can be worth it for personal injury firms when leads are exclusive and intake responds within minutes. Given typical contingency fees, a cost per signed case of a few thousand dollars can still be profitable on strong cases. It fails when leads are shared or follow-up is slow.

### What is the difference between pay per lead and pay per case?

Pay per lead charges for each delivered contact, while pay per case charges only when a prospect signs a retainer. Pay per case shifts conversion risk to the vendor and costs more per unit. Pay per lead is cheaper per unit but relies on your intake performance.

### Are exclusive leads better than shared leads?

Exclusive leads are generally better because only your firm receives the contact, removing the speed race against competitors. They cost more per lead but usually convert at higher rates. The lower cost per signed case often outweighs the higher sticker price.

### How do I know if a pay per lead vendor is compliant?

A compliant vendor can document consent for each lead, disclose where the lead originated, and show that outreach complies with TCPA and state privacy laws. Ask for sample consent language and lead-source records. If a vendor cannot provide them, treat that as a red flag.
