Most law firms have no idea whether their marketing is working. They know they're spending money — on Google Ads, on SEO agencies, on lead generation services — but they can't draw a clear line between those dollars and the revenue they produce.

This isn't just a reporting problem. It's a strategic one. Without clear **law firm marketing ROI** data, you can't make informed decisions about where to invest, what to cut, and how to grow. You're steering a business with the dashboard lights off.

The good news: measuring legal marketing ROI is simpler than most firms think. It doesn't require a data science team or enterprise analytics software. It requires discipline, a few key metrics, and a willingness to let the numbers guide your decisions.

## Why ROI Measurement Is Hard for Law Firms

Before we dive into how, it's worth understanding why so many firms struggle with this.

**Long revenue cycles.** A PI case signed today might not settle for 18 to 24 months. That means the marketing dollars you spent in January might not produce revenue until the following year. This lag makes it difficult to connect marketing spend to outcomes in real time.

**Multiple touchpoints.** A client might see your TV ad, Google your firm, read a blog post, and then call after getting a referral from a friend. Which of those touchpoints "caused" the case? Attribution is genuinely complex.

**Inconsistent tracking.** Most firms don't have systems in place to track where leads come from, let alone where signed cases originated. Without source tracking, ROI calculation is impossible.

**Fee variability.** Unlike e-commerce where a sale has a known value, legal cases range from a few thousand dollars to millions. This variance makes averages misleading and requires more sophisticated analysis.

## The Metrics That Matter

Forget impressions, clicks, and website visits. Those are inputs, not outcomes. Here are the metrics that actually tell you whether your marketing is working:

### Cost Per Lead (CPL)

Total marketing spend divided by total leads generated. This is your top-of-funnel efficiency metric.

**Why it matters:** CPL tells you how efficiently your marketing dollars are generating opportunities. But it's not sufficient on its own — a low CPL means nothing if the leads don't convert.

**Benchmark:** Legal CPL varies enormously by practice area and channel. PI leads from Google Ads might cost $150 to $300 each. Data-sourced leads might cost $50 to $150. Organic leads from SEO are "free" in terms of marginal cost but require significant upfront investment.

### Cost Per Signed Case (CPSC)

Total marketing spend divided by total cases signed. This is the metric that bridges marketing and revenue.

**Why it matters:** CPSC accounts for your entire funnel — not just how many leads you generate, but how many you actually convert. A firm with expensive leads but a high close rate might have a lower CPSC than a firm with cheap leads and poor intake.

**How to improve it:** There are two levers. Generate better-qualified leads (which improves close rates) or [optimize your intake process](/blog/legal-client-intake) to convert more of the leads you already have. Ideally, do both.

### Return on Marketing Investment (ROMI)

Revenue generated from marketing-sourced cases divided by total marketing spend. Expressed as a ratio or percentage.

**Why it matters:** This is the bottom line. A ROMI of 5:1 means every dollar you spend on marketing generates five dollars in revenue. For most PI firms, a healthy ROMI target is between 5:1 and 10:1.

**The complexity:** Because of the revenue cycle lag mentioned above, true ROMI can only be calculated retroactively. But you can estimate it in real time using average case values and historical close rates.

### Lifetime Client Value (LCV)

The total revenue a client generates over the full duration of the relationship, including referrals and repeat business.

**Why it matters:** Firms that only measure first-case revenue systematically undervalue their marketing. A workers' comp client who returns with a PI case three years later — that revenue should be attributed to the original marketing spend. ## Building an Attribution Model

Attribution answers the question: which marketing activity generated this case?

### First-Touch Attribution

Credits the first marketing interaction the client had with your firm. If they first found you through a Google search, organic search gets the credit.

**Best for:** Understanding which channels are best at generating awareness and new prospects.

### Last-Touch Attribution

Credits the final interaction before the client contacted your firm. If they called after clicking a Google Ad, paid search gets the credit.

**Best for:** Understanding which channels are best at driving action.

### Multi-Touch Attribution

Distributes credit across all touchpoints in the client journey. More complex to implement but more accurate for firms using multiple marketing channels.

**Best for:** Firms spending significant budget across three or more channels.

For most law firms just starting with attribution, **last-touch is the pragmatic choice**. It's the easiest to implement and it captures the channel that most directly drove the conversion. You can layer in multi-touch sophistication later.

## Practical Implementation

Here's how to start measuring ROI at your firm this month:

### Step 1: Track Lead Sources

Every lead that enters your pipeline needs a source tag. Where did they come from? Google Ads? Organic search? Referral? Semperr? Your lead generation partner?

If you use a CRM (Clio Grow, Lawmatics, Lead Docket), this should be a required field. If you're tracking manually, add a "How did you hear about us?" question to your intake form and follow-up calls.

### Step 2: Track Conversions

A lead isn't a case. You need to track how many leads from each source actually convert to signed retainers. This requires your intake team to update lead records as they move through the pipeline.

### Step 3: Assign Revenue

When a case settles, attribute the revenue back to the original lead source. This is where the long cycle time creates challenges — you need a system that maintains the connection between marketing source and case outcome over months or years.

### Step 4: Calculate by Channel

With source tracking, conversion tracking, and revenue attribution in place, you can calculate CPL, CPSC, and ROMI for each channel individually. This tells you exactly where to increase investment and where to cut.

## Using the Data to Make Decisions

Once you have ROI data by channel, the decision framework is straightforward:

**Double down on winners.** If your [lead generation](/blog/law-firm-lead-generation) partner is delivering leads at a 7:1 ROMI and Google Ads is at 3:1, shift budget accordingly.

**Fix or cut losers.** A channel with poor ROI might need optimization, not elimination. But if you've optimized and it still underperforms, reallocate that budget.

**Test new channels with small budgets.** Don't bet the farm on an unproven channel. Allocate a small test budget, run it for 90 days, measure the results, and scale if the numbers work.

**Account for time lag.** Don't judge a new marketing initiative by its first month of results. Legal revenue cycles mean you need at least six months of data before drawing conclusions.
