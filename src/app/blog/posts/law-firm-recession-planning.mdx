Most firms treat a downturn as a single event: revenue drops, so spending drops. That framing is wrong, and it is expensive. **Law firm recession planning** works better when you treat a downturn as a rotation of demand, because some practice areas shrink while others grow faster than they do in good years.

The firms that struggle are the ones that cut marketing across the board, keep staffing built for last year's mix, and discover too late that their best-performing case type is the one consumers can no longer afford to pursue. The firms that hold steady decide in advance where demand will move.

## Why Law Firm Recession Planning Is a Case Mix Problem

Legal work is not uniformly cyclical. Transactional and discretionary matters tend to slow when credit tightens and consumers hold back. Matters triggered by financial stress, job loss, or disputes over unpaid money tend to speed up.

That split is the core of any sound plan. A firm that is 80 percent real estate transactions and a firm that is 80 percent debt-related work face opposite risks from the same economic event.

Three questions frame the exercise:

- Which of your case types depend on a client's willingness to spend discretionary money?
- Which depend on an event that happens regardless of the economy, such as an injury or an arrest?
- Which are triggered by financial distress and therefore tend to grow when conditions worsen?

## Practice Areas That Tend to Gain, Hold, or Lose

No practice area is recession-proof, and the pattern varies by cycle. Still, the broad direction is consistent enough to plan around.

### Areas that often gain demand

Consumer and business bankruptcy, debt defense, foreclosure defense, and employment disputes tend to rise when layoffs and credit stress rise. If you are weighing a move into this work, our guide to [bankruptcy lead generation](/blog/bankruptcy-lead-generation) covers how to reach people before they call someone else.

### Areas that tend to hold

Personal injury, workers' compensation, and criminal defense are driven by accidents and arrests, which do not follow the business cycle. Volume holds, but the economics shift. Clients who are financially stressed are more cautious about hiring, and contingency fee cases get more scrutiny from intake teams.

### Areas that tend to slow

Corporate transactions, real estate closings, and estate planning that people can postpone often slow. Firms heavily weighted here need a plan before volume drops, not after.

## Where Law Firm Recession Planning Breaks Down: Marketing Cuts

The most common mistake is pulling advertising budgets evenly. Acquisition cost does not stay constant while you wait. When competitors cut spend, auction pressure on paid search can ease, and **cost per case** can fall for the firms that keep bidding.

The reverse also happens. If you cut spend and then restart six months later, you rebuild campaign history, reviews, and local rankings from a weaker position.

A better approach to the budget is to cut by marginal return, not by percentage:

1. Rank every channel by cost per signed case, not cost per lead.
2. Cut the bottom quartile first, regardless of channel size.
3. Hold or increase spend on channels producing cases below your target cost.
4. Shift budget toward practice areas where demand is rising.

## Cash Flow Is the Real Recession Risk

Revenue declines rarely kill firms. Timing mismatches do. Contingency firms front case costs for months or years before a fee arrives, and a slowdown in settlements stretches that gap.

The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has repeatedly shown that collection and billing realization are persistent weak points for firms of all sizes. In a downturn, clients pay slower, which widens the problem for hourly and flat-fee practices.

Build a floor before you need it:

- Hold enough cash reserves to cover several months of fixed overhead.
- Negotiate a line of credit while your financials look strong, not after they weaken.
- Tighten billing cadence and payment plans for hourly and flat-fee matters.
- Review case cost advances and stop funding marginal cases.

## Staffing and Intake in a Downturn

Headcount is the largest fixed cost most firms carry, and it is the hardest to adjust. Layoffs also carry a hidden cost: you lose the intake and case management experience you will need when volume returns.

Prefer reversible moves. Slow hiring, shift work toward growing practice areas, and cross-train staff before you consider cuts. Protect intake above almost everything else, because when lead volume tightens, conversion rate becomes the main lever on revenue. The [Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html) publishes market reports that track demand and expense pressures across the profession and are worth reviewing alongside your own numbers.

## Build a Trigger-Based Plan

A plan that depends on someone noticing a problem is not a plan. Define the signals and the response in advance.

Useful triggers include:

- Lead volume or signed cases falling a set percentage below trailing averages for two consecutive months.
- Cost per signed case rising above a fixed ceiling for a channel.
- Average collection time stretching beyond a set number of days.
- Case advances exceeding a percentage of cash reserves.

Attach a specific action to each trigger, such as pausing a channel, freezing hiring, or moving budget to a growing practice area. Review the triggers quarterly. ## Keep Your Pipeline Predictable

In a slow market, volatility in lead flow is the thing that hurts most. A pipeline built on a single channel or a single case type leaves you exposed to every swing. Diversifying across channels and practice areas, and buying exclusive, pre-qualified demand where it fits, gives you a floor under signed cases while you adjust.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is law firm recession planning?

Law firm recession planning is the process of preparing a practice for reduced demand and tighter cash before a downturn arrives. It covers case mix, marketing budget allocation, cash reserves, staffing, and predefined triggers for action. The goal is to avoid reactive across-the-board cuts.

### Which practice areas do well in a recession?

Practice areas tied to financial distress, such as bankruptcy, debt defense, foreclosure defense, and employment disputes, often see rising demand. Personal injury, workers' compensation, and criminal defense tend to hold steady because accidents and arrests do not follow the economy. Transactional and discretionary work usually slows.

### Should law firms cut marketing during a recession?

Law firms should cut marketing selectively, not evenly. Rank channels by cost per signed case, remove the weakest first, and protect channels producing cases below your target cost. Competitors often pull back, which can lower acquisition costs for firms that keep spending.

### How much cash should a law firm keep in reserve?

A common rule of thumb is several months of fixed overhead, with contingency firms holding more to cover case cost advances. The right figure depends on your fee mix, collection speed, and credit access. Review it against your [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/) practice management resources and your own cash flow history.
