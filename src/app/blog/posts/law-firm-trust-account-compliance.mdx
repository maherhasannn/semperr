Lawyers rarely lose their licenses over bad marketing. They lose them over money. Trust account compliance is the area of professional responsibility where an honest bookkeeping mistake and outright theft can look identical on a bank statement, and where regulators are least interested in hearing about intent.

The uncomfortable part is that growth makes the problem worse. Every new signed case eventually puts client money in your hands: an advance fee, a cost deposit, a settlement check. A firm that doubles its intake doubles the number of client ledgers it must keep straight, usually with the same part-time bookkeeper and the same spreadsheet.

## What Trust Account Compliance Actually Requires

The foundation is [ABA Model Rule 1.15](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_15_safekeeping_property/), which every state has adopted in some form. Strip away the commentary and it imposes five duties:

- **Separation.** Client and third-party funds must be held in an account separate from the lawyer's own property.
- **Records.** Complete records of the account must be kept and preserved after the representation ends. The Model Rule says five years; several states require longer.
- **Advance fees and costs.** Legal fees and expenses paid in advance go into trust and come out only as fees are earned or expenses incurred.
- **Prompt notice and delivery.** When funds arrive in which a client or third party has an interest, you must notify them promptly, deliver what they are owed, and provide an accounting on request.
- **Disputed funds.** If two parties claim the same money, including you and your client, the disputed portion stays in trust until the dispute is resolved.

The rule permits exactly one deposit of the lawyer's own money: an amount needed to cover bank service charges on the account. Anything beyond that is commingling.

### Where IOLTA fits

Funds that are nominal in amount or held for a short time go into a pooled Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts account, with the interest paid to a state foundation that funds legal aid. Larger or longer-held sums may need a separate interest-bearing account for the benefit of that client.

IOLTA account rules vary by state on which banks are eligible, how interest is remitted, and what the lawyer must certify each year. The state-specific version, not the Model Rule, is the one that governs your license.

## The Violations That Catch Honest Firms

Disciplinary reports are full of lawyers who never intended to take a dollar. The patterns repeat:

1. **Commingling client funds with operating money.** Leaving earned fees in trust "as a cushion" is as much a violation as depositing a settlement check into the operating account. Earned fees must move out promptly.
2. **Disbursing against uncollected funds.** A settlement check is deposited Monday and the client is paid Tuesday. If the deposit has not cleared, you just paid one client with another client's money.
3. **Negative client ledgers.** The overall account balance is positive, but one client's sub-ledger is overdrawn. The bank will not flag it. An auditor will.
4. **Processing fees and chargebacks hitting trust.** A card processor that deducts its fee from the trust deposit, or pulls a chargeback from it, is taking other clients' money. Fees must be debited from operating.
5. **Treating "nonrefundable" as "earned."** ABA Formal Opinion 505 confirmed in 2023 that fees paid in advance for future work belong in trust until earned, whatever the fee agreement calls them.
6. **Stale balances.** Small leftover amounts on closed files sit for years. They are still client property, and eventually they are an unclaimed property problem too.

None of these trust account compliance failures require bad faith. They require only volume and inattention, which is exactly what a fast-growing firm produces.

## Why Trust Account Compliance Breaks at Scale

A solo with 40 open files can carry the state of the trust account in their head. A firm with 400 cannot, and the failure modes change as the count rises.

### More cases, more ledgers, more disbursements

Contingency practices feel this most. Each settlement produces a deposit, a fee transfer, a cost reimbursement, lien and subrogation payments, and a client disbursement. Ten settlements a month means fifty or more trust transactions, each tied to a specific ledger.

The pressure compounds because clients want their money fast and the firm wants its fee. Firms already stretched by the gap described in our guide to [law firm cash flow management](/blog/law-firm-cash-flow-management) are the ones most tempted to transfer fees before a check clears.

### Flat fees and online payments

Practice areas that run on flat or advance fees, such as criminal defense, immigration, and bankruptcy, face a different version. Dozens of small payments arrive weekly by card, ACH, and payment link.

Each one has to be routed correctly at the moment of payment: unearned money to trust, earned money to operating, processing fees to operating always. Firms experimenting with [alternative fee arrangements](/blog/law-firm-alternative-fee-arrangements) need to decide where each payment lands before they publish the price, not after.

### Signature authority outgrows the owner

At some point the managing partner stops signing every check. Delegation is necessary, but the duty is not delegable. Model Rule 5.3 makes the lawyer responsible for nonlawyer staff, and "my bookkeeper did it" has never worked as a defense.

Embezzlement by a trusted employee is one of the most common ways a clean firm ends up in front of a disciplinary board. The lawyer is typically sanctioned for failing to supervise even when they are also the victim.

## Three-Way Reconciliation: The Control Regulators Look For First

If you build only one trust account compliance control, build this one. It compares three numbers that must match to the penny:

- **The adjusted bank balance.** The statement balance, adjusted for outstanding checks and deposits in transit.
- **The trust account book balance.** The running balance in your check register or accounting system.
- **The sum of all client ledgers.** Every individual client balance added together.

When the three agree, every dollar in the bank is assigned to a specific client. When they do not, something is wrong, and you have found it within a month instead of during an investigation.

The ABA Model Rules for Client Trust Account Records call for reconciliation at least quarterly. Many states, including California and Florida, require it monthly, and monthly is the only defensible cadence for a firm with real volume.

Two details separate a real reconciliation from a ritual. First, a lawyer with responsibility for the account must review and sign it, not just the person who prepared it. Second, the report must list each client balance individually, so a negative ledger cannot hide inside a healthy total.

## A Client Trust Accounting System That Survives Growth

Sound client trust accounting is less about software than about separation of duties and written rules. The controls below scale from five people to fifty.

1. **Split the functions.** The person who records transactions should not be the person who reconciles, and neither should be the only one who sees the bank statement. In a small office, have statements delivered unopened to a partner.
2. **Write a clearance policy.** Define how many business days each deposit type must sit before disbursement, and do not allow exceptions for favorite clients or large fees.
3. **Require a ledger check before every disbursement.** No payment leaves trust unless that client's ledger covers it. Practice management systems can block this automatically; use the setting.
4. **Configure payment processors correctly.** Use a processor built for legal trust accounting that pulls fees and chargebacks from operating. Confirm it in writing, the same way you would handle any item in a [vendor risk management](/blog/law-firm-vendor-risk-management) review.
5. **Document settlement disbursements.** Every settlement gets a signed closing statement showing gross recovery, fees, costs, liens, and the net to the client. Storing signed copies ties neatly into your [e-signature compliance](/blog/law-firm-e-signature-compliance) process.
6. **Sweep stale balances quarterly.** Run a report of ledgers with no activity in 90 days and resolve each one.
7. **Put it in an SOP.** A trust procedure that lives in one person's memory disappears when they resign. It belongs in your [standard operating procedures](/blog/law-firm-standard-operating-procedures), with a named backup for every step.

Guard against wire fraud as part of the same system. Disbursement instructions that arrive or change by email should be verified by a phone call to a known number before any wire is sent.

## What Triggers a Trust Account Audit

Firms tend to assume regulators only test trust account compliance after a client complaint. In most states there are several other paths.

- **Overdraft notification.** The large majority of states require banks holding lawyer trust accounts to report any overdraft or dishonored instrument directly to the disciplinary authority, even if it was covered the same day.
- **Random audits.** A number of states, New Jersey and North Carolina among them, run random audit programs that select firms with no complaint on file.
- **Annual certification.** Many bars require lawyers to certify compliance on their annual registration. California's Client Trust Account Protection Program now requires yearly account registration and a compliance self-assessment.
- **Unrelated complaints.** A grievance about communication or fees frequently leads investigators to request trust records as a matter of routine.

An examination is far less frightening for a firm that can produce twelve months of signed reconciliations in an afternoon. Firms that cannot produce them tend to be presumed out of compliance, and the burden of reconstructing records falls on the lawyer.

The [ABA Center for Professional Responsibility](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/) maintains state-by-state resources on recordkeeping and overdraft rules. Check your own jurisdiction's version before relying on any general summary, including this one.

## Trust Account Compliance Belongs in Your Growth Plan

Most firms budget for the marketing that brings in cases and the staff that works them. Very few budget for the back-office capacity that handles the money those cases produce.

A useful rule: any plan that increases signed cases should include a line for trust account compliance. That may mean upgrading software, adding bookkeeper hours, or bringing in an outside accountant for monthly reconciliation review.

Technology adoption helps here. The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has tracked the steady shift toward electronic payments in legal services, and firms that accept them through purpose-built legal payment tools get correct routing by default instead of by memory.

The cost of getting trust account compliance right is modest. The cost of getting it wrong is measured in suspensions.

Predictable case volume makes trust accounting easier, not harder. When intake arrives in erratic bursts, deposits and disbursements bunch up, staff rush, and controls get skipped. When volume is steady and forecastable, you can staff the function properly and keep every ledger clean.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is trust account compliance for a law firm?

Trust account compliance means holding client and third-party money according to your state's version of Rule 1.15. In practice that requires keeping client funds separate from firm funds, maintaining a ledger for every client, reconciling the account regularly, and disbursing money only when it is cleared and owed. Violations can lead to discipline even when no client loses money.

### How often should a law firm perform a three-way reconciliation?

A law firm should perform a three-way reconciliation every month. The ABA model recordkeeping rule sets a quarterly minimum, but many states require monthly reconciliation and monthly statements make errors far easier to trace. A responsible lawyer should review and sign each one.

### What counts as commingling client funds?

Commingling client funds means mixing client money with the lawyer's own money in the same account. It includes depositing client funds into an operating account, leaving earned fees in trust, and paying firm expenses directly from the trust account. The only firm money allowed in trust is a small amount to cover bank service charges.

### What triggers a trust account audit?

A trust account audit is most often triggered by a bank overdraft notice, a client complaint, or selection in a state random audit program. Most states require banks to report trust account overdrafts directly to the disciplinary authority. Investigators also commonly request trust records while looking into unrelated grievances.

### Do IOLTA account rules allow credit card payments into trust?

IOLTA account rules in most states allow credit card payments into trust, provided processing fees and chargebacks are never taken from client funds. The safest setup uses a legal-specific processor that deposits the full payment to trust and debits all fees from the operating account. Confirm your state's position, since a few jurisdictions impose additional conditions.
