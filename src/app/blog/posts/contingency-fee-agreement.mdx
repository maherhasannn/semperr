Most plaintiff firms are still signing clients on a contingency fee agreement somebody drafted a decade ago. It has been copied between practice areas, patched after one bad dispute, and loaded into an e-signature tool without anyone reading it end to end. Nobody looks at it again until a client fires the firm, challenges the settlement math, or files a bar complaint.

That is the wrong moment to discover a gap. In several states, a fee contract that omits a required term is voidable at the client's option, and the firm is left arguing for a "reasonable fee" on a case it financed for two years. The document that feels like routine paperwork is the only thing standing between your firm and that argument.

## What a Contingency Fee Agreement Must Contain

The baseline is [ABA Model Rule 1.5](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_5_fees/). Every state has adopted its own version, and several add requirements on top, so treat the model text as the floor for any contingency fee agreement.

Rule 1.5(c) is unusually specific for an ethics rule. It requires a **written fee agreement signed by the client**, and it lists what the writing must say:

- **The method for determining the fee**, including the percentage or percentages that apply if the matter settles, goes to trial, or is appealed
- **Which litigation and other expenses** will be deducted from the recovery
- **Whether those expenses come out before or after** the fee is calculated
- **Any expenses the client will owe** whether or not the client wins

The last item is the one most often missing. If your contingency fee agreement says the client owes nothing unless there is a recovery, but your practice is to bill certain costs regardless, the document and the practice disagree. The client will be held to the document.

Rule 1.5(a) sits over all of it. A fee can comply with every drafting requirement and still be unreasonable given the time, risk, and result. A 40 percent fee on a policy-limits claim that settled with one demand letter is the classic example regulators cite.

### Where a contingent fee is not allowed at all

Rule 1.5(d) bars contingent fees in two settings: criminal defense, and domestic relations matters where the fee depends on securing a divorce or on the amount of support or property settlement. Firms expanding from personal injury into family law sometimes carry their template across without noticing.

Collection of past-due support that has already been ordered is treated differently in many states. Check your jurisdiction's comment to the rule before assuming either way.

## Net or Gross: The Expense Clause That Changes the Client's Check

The rule requires you to say whether expenses are deducted before or after the fee is calculated because the difference is real money. Most clients never understand it until the disbursement sheet arrives.

Take a $100,000 settlement, $10,000 in advanced costs, and a one-third fee:

1. **Fee on the gross recovery.** The fee is $33,333. Costs of $10,000 come out next. The client receives $56,667.
2. **Fee on the net recovery.** Costs come out first, leaving $90,000. The fee is $30,000. The client receives $60,000.

That is a $3,333 difference on a modest case. On a litigated trucking or medical malpractice matter with six figures in expert costs, the gap can exceed the client's expectations by tens of thousands of dollars.

Either method is generally permitted. What is not permitted is silence or ambiguity, because ambiguous fee terms are construed against the lawyer who drafted them. State the method in one plain sentence and include a worked example in the contingency fee agreement itself.

Define "expenses" with the same care. Filing fees and expert witnesses are obvious. Medical record retrieval charges, lien resolution vendors, litigation funding interest, and in-house copying are where disputes start. [Model Rule 1.8](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_8_current_clients_specific_rules/) lets a lawyer advance court costs and litigation expenses with repayment contingent on the outcome, but it does not let you invent new categories after the fact.

If your firm uses outside capital to carry case costs, the agreement should say whether financing charges are passed through to the client. The answer varies by state, and we covered the underlying economics in our piece on [third-party litigation funding](/blog/third-party-litigation-funding-law-firms).

## Contingency Fee Caps Override Whatever Your Agreement Says

A signed contingency fee agreement does not beat a statute. A surprising number of firms run multi-state campaigns with a single contingency fee percentage and learn about local limits only when a judge or opposing counsel raises them.

A sample of the limits that catch firms:

- **Federal Tort Claims Act.** Fees are capped at 25 percent of a judgment or post-suit settlement, and 20 percent of an administrative settlement. Charging more is a federal offense, not just an ethics problem.
- **California medical malpractice.** After the 2022 amendments to MICRA, the cap is 25 percent if the recovery comes before a complaint or arbitration demand is filed, and 33 percent after.
- **Florida.** The bar rules set a presumptive schedule for personal injury matters, starting at one-third of recoveries up to $1 million before an answer is filed and 40 percent after, with lower percentages on higher tiers. The client must also receive and sign the Statement of Client's Rights.
- **New Jersey.** Court rule sets a sliding scale that begins at one-third of the first tier of the net recovery and steps down from there, with court approval needed above the top tier.
- **New York medical malpractice.** A statutory sliding scale starts at 30 percent of the first tier and falls to 10 percent on amounts above $1.25 million.
- **Workers' compensation and Social Security disability.** Fees are set or approved by the agency, and private agreements cannot exceed those limits.

These figures change, and the tiers have exceptions. Verify the current text in each state where you sign clients, and assign one person to own that review annually.

These limits matter most to firms buying leads across state lines. If your intake team signs a Florida client on a template built for Texas, the missing client-rights statement alone can put the fee at risk. ### State formalities that make an agreement voidable

Beyond the percentage, several states impose formal requirements that carry a harsh remedy.

California is the clearest example. Its Business and Professions Code requires that the client receive a signed duplicate of the contract, and that the contract include specific statements, among them that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable in matters outside the medical malpractice cap. Miss a required element and the agreement is **voidable at the client's option**, leaving the lawyer to seek a reasonable fee instead.

Texas requires a contingent fee contract to be in writing and signed by both the attorney and the client. Parts of New York require personal injury firms to file retainer and closing statements with the court system. Florida gives the client a short window to cancel after signing.

Signature mechanics matter here too. An agreement that satisfies every content rule still fails if you cannot prove who signed it, when, and what version they saw. ## The Settlement Closing Statement Completes the Contingency Fee Agreement

Rule 1.5(c) does not end at signing. When a contingent matter concludes, the lawyer must give the client a written statement showing the outcome and, if there is a recovery, the remittance to the client and how it was calculated.

A proper settlement closing statement shows:

- The gross recovery and its source
- The fee, with the percentage and the base it was applied to
- Each expense, itemized, not lumped as "costs"
- Liens and subrogation claims paid, and any reductions negotiated
- The net amount to the client

This is where the agreement gets tested. If the statement applies the fee to the gross and the contract says net, the mismatch is on paper, signed by your firm. Every figure should trace back to a clause in the fee contract and a ledger entry in the trust account.

Funds stay in trust until the statement is delivered and any disputed portion is resolved, as [Model Rule 1.15](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_15_safekeeping_property/) requires. Firms that sweep fees out the day the check clears, then send the statement a week later, are creating a problem that has nothing to do with the fee itself. ## Clauses That Protect the Fee When the Relationship Ends

Clients change lawyers. In high-volume practices built on advertising and purchased leads, a meaningful share of signed clients will be contacted by another firm before the case resolves. A well-drafted contingency fee agreement anticipates that.

Four clauses do most of the work:

1. **Discharge and withdrawal.** State what the firm is owed if the client terminates before recovery. Most states limit a discharged lawyer to quantum meruit, sometimes capped by the contract percentage, so a clause demanding the full fee is unenforceable and makes the document look overreaching.
2. **Scope of representation.** Say whether appeals, lien litigation, probate of a wrongful death settlement, and collection of a judgment are included. Unstated scope is read broadly against the firm.
3. **Fee division with co-counsel.** If another firm will share the fee, Rule 1.5(e) generally requires the client's written agreement to the arrangement, including the share each lawyer receives. Build that consent into the signing packet, not a later email.
4. **Settlement authority.** The decision to settle belongs to the client. Clauses that let the firm settle without consent, or penalize the client for rejecting an offer, are improper in most jurisdictions.

A structured-settlement clause is worth adding for larger cases. It should say whether the fee is calculated on present value or on the total of future payments, and when it is paid.

## Auditing Your Contingency Fee Agreement Before You Scale Intake

Template drift is the real risk. One version lives in the e-signature tool, another in the case management system, and a third in a paralegal's desktop folder. When volume doubles, the wrong one gets sent more often.

A workable audit takes an afternoon:

- Pull every version currently in use and retire all but one per state and practice area
- Check each against the state's version of Rule 1.5 and any fee statute or court rule
- Confirm the expense method in the contract matches what accounting actually does
- Compare ten recent closing statements against the agreements those clients signed
- Confirm intake staff cannot edit percentages or delete clauses in the signing tool
- Record who reviewed the template and when

Then walk through how the agreement is presented. A client who signs on a phone ninety seconds after a first call, with no explanation of fees or costs, is the client who later says they never understood the terms. A two-minute scripted explanation, documented in the file, is cheap insurance and improves the [client onboarding process](/blog/law-firm-client-onboarding-process) at the same time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What must a contingency fee agreement include?

A contingency fee agreement must be in writing, signed by the client, and state how the fee is calculated. Under Rule 1.5(c), that means the percentages at settlement, trial, and appeal, the expenses to be deducted, whether they come out before or after the fee, and any costs the client owes regardless of outcome. Many states add their own required disclosures.

### What is a typical contingency fee percentage?

A typical contingency fee percentage in personal injury matters is one-third of the recovery before suit and around 40 percent once litigation begins. The number is not uniform, because statutes and court rules cap it in certain case types and states. Whatever the percentage, the fee must still be reasonable under Rule 1.5(a).

### Which states have contingency fee caps?

Contingency fee caps exist in many states, most often for medical malpractice, workers' compensation, and claims against government entities. California, Florida, New Jersey, and New York all have schedules or sliding scales, and federal law caps fees in Federal Tort Claims Act and Social Security matters. The limits change, so confirm the current rule in every state where you sign clients.

### Can a client cancel a contingency fee agreement?

Yes, a client can discharge a lawyer at any time, with or without cause. In most states the discharged lawyer is limited to the reasonable value of the work performed, payable only if the client later recovers. Some states also give the client a short statutory window to cancel after signing with no fee owed.

### Is a written fee agreement required for every case?

A written fee agreement is mandatory for contingent matters under Rule 1.5(c) and strongly recommended for everything else. For hourly and flat-fee work, the model rule only says the fee terms should preferably be in writing, though some states require a writing above a dollar threshold. A signed document is the firm's best evidence in any fee dispute.
