Most contingency firms set next year's budget by looking at last year's deposits and adding a hopeful percentage. That is not a forecast. It is a guess with a spreadsheet attached, and it is why **law firm revenue forecasting** has a reputation for being impossible in practices where a single settlement can swing a quarter.

It is not impossible. The fees a firm will collect over the next 12 months are, for the most part, already sitting in its open files. The cases are signed, the injuries are documented, and the coverage is known. What is missing is a method for turning that inventory into a number with a date on it.

## Why Law Firm Revenue Forecasting Fails at Most Contingency Firms

Hourly firms forecast from billable capacity: timekeepers, rates, realization. Contingency firms have none of those anchors, so many of them conclude that forecasting does not apply to them.

The real problems are narrower and fixable. They tend to be the same four everywhere:

- **Forecasting from the bank statement.** Last year's collections reflect cases signed 12 to 30 months ago. They say little about the inventory you hold today.
- **Treating every open file as equal.** A file signed last week and a file with a demand out are both "open," but they are not remotely the same asset.
- **Relying on attorney optimism.** Ask a lawyer what a case is worth and you get the best plausible outcome, not the expected one.
- **Ignoring timing.** A fee that arrives in March and a fee that arrives in October fund very different hiring and marketing decisions.

There is also a cultural obstacle. Partners who built a firm on instinct often resist a model that will be visibly wrong some months. A forecast does not need to be right every month to be useful; it needs to be less wrong than the guess it replaces.

## The Three Inputs Behind Every Contingency Fee Revenue Forecast

Strip away the software and every credible projection of **contingency fee revenue** rests on three numbers per case: what it is likely to pay, how likely it is to pay at all, and when.

### Expected fee

Start with historical averages, not individual case opinions. Pull every case closed in the last two to three years and calculate the median and average net fee by case type and resolution path. A pre-litigation auto claim, a litigated premises case, and a workers' compensation file each get their own figure.

Use those averages for early-stage files. Replace them with a case-specific estimate only once there is hard information: known policy limits, complete medical specials, or an offer on the table.

### Probability of a fee

Not every signed case pays. Some are dropped after investigation, some clients disappear, and some lose. Your closed-file data will tell you the share of cases that reached each stage and ultimately produced a fee.

That share rises as a case advances. A newly signed file might historically convert to a fee far less often than one in active negotiation. Use your own numbers here; borrowing another firm's rates is how forecasts go wrong.

### Expected timing

This is the input most firms skip. For each stage, measure the median number of days from entering that stage to fee received. If you have already done the stage-by-stage work described in our guide to [law firm case cycle time](/blog/law-firm-case-cycle-time), you have this data in hand.

**Settlement timing** is what converts a pile of expected fees into a monthly projection. Without it you know roughly how much, but not whether it lands before payroll.

## Building a Weighted Pipeline: A Worked Example

The core of law firm revenue forecasting is the same method sales organizations have used for decades: multiply value by probability, then place the result in the month you expect it.

Here is a simplified, illustrative snapshot of a pre-litigation personal injury inventory. The figures are a model, not a benchmark.

| Stage | Open files | Avg. expected fee | Probability | Weighted fees | Median days to fee |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signed, in treatment | 120 | $8,000 | 60% | $576,000 | 330 |
| Records and demand prep | 60 | $8,500 | 75% | $382,500 | 180 |
| Demand out, negotiating | 45 | $9,000 | 88% | $356,400 | 90 |
| Settled, awaiting funds | 20 | $9,500 | 98% | $186,200 | 30 |

The total **case pipeline value** in this model is about $1.5 million. More useful is the shape: roughly $186,000 should arrive within a month, another $356,000 within about a quarter, and the largest block is nearly a year out.

Three practical rules make a weighted pipeline hold up:

1. **Forecast in ranges.** Publish a low, expected, and high case. A single number invites false confidence.
2. **Pull large cases out of the averages.** Any file with an expected fee several times your median should be forecast individually and shown on its own line, so one delayed mediation does not wreck the model.
3. **Add a layer for cases not yet signed.** Files you will sign in the next few months can pay within the forecast window, especially fast-resolving types. Estimate them from planned intake volume and historical conversion.

That third layer is where marketing and finance meet. If you know your signed-case volume by source, the forecast can show what a change in acquisition spend does to fees 9 to 15 months later.

## Turning Law Firm Revenue Forecasting Into Budget Decisions

A forecast that lives in a spreadsheet nobody opens is a hobby. The point of law firm revenue forecasting is to change what the firm commits to and when.

### Hiring

Headcount is the largest fixed commitment most firms make. A pipeline that shows inventory building in early stages tells you case manager capacity will be needed before the fees to pay for it arrive. Our guide to [law firm hiring strategy](/blog/law-firm-hiring-strategy) covers how to time those hires; the forecast tells you whether you can afford to be early.

### Marketing spend

Acquisition dollars spent today return as fees a year or more later. Sound **law firm budgeting** sets marketing spend against forecast collections rather than last month's deposits, which prevents the familiar cycle of cutting ads after a slow quarter and starving the pipeline a year later. For the allocation side, see our breakdown of the [law firm marketing budget](/blog/law-firm-marketing-budget).

### Financing and reserves

Lenders and litigation funders respond far better to a documented pipeline than to a verbal assurance that "a few big ones are about to settle." A forecast also shows the trough months in advance, when a credit line is cheap to arrange. The mechanics of bridging those gaps are covered in our piece on [law firm cash flow management](/blog/law-firm-cash-flow-management).

### Partner distributions

Distributing a windfall quarter in full and then borrowing to cover the next one is common and avoidable. A rolling 12-month view makes it obvious how much of a strong month is surplus and how much is simply timing.

The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has repeatedly shown how much firm revenue is lost between work performed and cash collected. Contingency firms face a longer version of the same gap, and a forecast is the only way to see it coming.

## The Mistakes That Wreck Law Firm Financial Projections

Even firms that commit to law firm revenue forecasting often abandon the model within two quarters. The causes are predictable.

- **Stale stage data.** If case managers do not update stages, the model is forecasting last month's firm. Make stage changes mandatory in your case management system.
- **Counting gross settlements instead of net fees.** Forecast the fee after referral splits and co-counsel shares. Advanced costs recovered are reimbursement, not revenue.
- **One probability for everything.** A firm-wide conversion rate hides the fact that some case types and lead sources drop out at twice the rate of others.
- **Never checking the forecast against reality.** Each month, compare projected collections to actual and record the variance. That comparison is how the probabilities and timing assumptions improve.
- **Forgetting the calendar.** Carriers, courts, and clients all slow down at predictable times of year, and a model that ignores those months will miss in the same direction every year.

A reasonable accuracy target for a first-year model is modest. If quarterly collections land within the low-to-high range most of the time, the model is doing its job. Monthly precision comes later, if at all.

Keep the forecast separate from trust accounting. Client funds in trust are not firm revenue until fees are earned and properly disbursed, and **law firm financial projections** should only ever count the firm's share.

## A 60-Day Plan to Stand Up Your First Forecast

You do not need a finance hire or new software to start law firm revenue forecasting. Most of the required data is already in your case management system and your closed-file records.

1. **Days 1-10: Define stages.** Agree on five to seven stages with a clear trigger event for each. Reuse the stages you already track if you have them.
2. **Days 11-25: Build the baseline.** From two to three years of closed cases, calculate average net fee, fee probability, and median days to fee for each stage and case type.
3. **Days 26-35: Apply it to open inventory.** Assign every open file a stage, multiply through, and place weighted fees into months. Break out large cases individually.
4. **Days 36-45: Add future signings.** Layer in expected new cases from planned intake volume.
5. **Days 46-60: Review with partners and set a cadence.** Update monthly, compare to actuals, and adjust assumptions quarterly.

Tie the output to the handful of numbers leadership already watches. If the forecast sits beside the metrics in your [law firm KPIs](/blog/law-firm-kpis-to-track) dashboard, it gets used; if it sits in a separate file, it gets forgotten.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is law firm revenue forecasting?

Law firm revenue forecasting is the process of estimating the fees a firm will collect over a future period, usually the next 12 months. For contingency practices, it means assigning each open case an expected fee, a probability of payment, and an expected collection date. The result is a monthly projection the firm can use to plan hiring, marketing, and distributions.

### How do you forecast contingency fee revenue when case outcomes are uncertain?

You forecast the portfolio, not the individual case. Any single file is unpredictable, but averages across hundreds of closed cases are stable enough to estimate fee size, drop-off rates, and timing by stage. Large or unusual cases are pulled out and forecast separately so they do not distort the averages.

### How accurate should law firm financial projections be?

Quarterly collections that fall inside a stated low-to-high range most of the time is a realistic first-year standard. Month-level precision is rarely achievable in contingency work because a few settlements can shift by weeks. Accuracy improves as the firm compares forecast to actual each month and corrects its assumptions.

### How is case pipeline value calculated?

Case pipeline value is the sum of each open file's expected net fee multiplied by its probability of producing a fee. A case with an expected fee of $9,000 and an 88 percent historical chance of payment contributes $7,920. Adding the expected collection date to each file turns that total into a month-by-month forecast.

### How often should a law firm update its revenue forecast?

Monthly is the right cadence for most firms. Update stages and expected dates, compare the prior month's projection to actual collections, and note the variance. Revisit the underlying probabilities and timing assumptions once a quarter, or sooner after a major change in case mix or lead sources.
