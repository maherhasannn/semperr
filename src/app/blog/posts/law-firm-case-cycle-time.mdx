Ask a managing partner how many cases the firm signed last month and you will get a number immediately. Ask how many days the average file takes to go from signed retainer to fee in the operating account and you will usually get a guess. That second number, **case cycle time**, shapes revenue as much as the first one does.

A contingency firm is an inventory business. Every open file is capital that has been spent on acquisition and staff time and has not yet come back. Shortening case cycle time returns that capital sooner, frees capacity for new matters, and does it without a single extra dollar of marketing spend.

## What Case Cycle Time Actually Measures

Case cycle time is the number of days between the signed retainer and the day the fee is received. Not the day the case settles. The day the money arrives.

That distinction matters because a large share of delay sits after the handshake: release paperwork, lien resolution, check clearance, and disbursement. A firm that stops the clock at settlement is ignoring weeks it can control.

A single firm-wide average is close to useless. A pre-litigation soft-tissue claim and a litigated trucking case have nothing in common, and blending them hides both. Track case cycle time separately for at least these groups:

- **Case type** (auto, premises, workers' compensation, and so on)
- **Resolution path** (pre-litigation settlement versus filed suit)
- **Handling team or attorney**
- **Lead source**, because some sources send cases that resolve cleanly and some do not

Use the median alongside the average. A handful of three-year files will distort an average badly, while the median tells you what a typical client experiences.

## Why Case Cycle Time Drives Growth More Than Signed Volume

Consider an illustrative firm with 300 open pre-litigation files, an average fee of $8,000, and a 15-month cycle. It resolves about 240 cases a year, which is roughly $1.9 million in fees.

Bring that cycle down to 12 months with the same staff and the same inventory, and the firm resolves about 300 cases a year. That is $2.4 million. **A 20 percent reduction in cycle time produced a 25 percent increase in annual fees**, with no change in marketing budget or headcount.

The numbers are a simplified model, but the mechanism is real, and it compounds in three ways.

### Capital comes back sooner

Every case carries acquisition cost and advanced expenses until it resolves. Slow cases are the reason profitable firms still run short of cash, a problem covered in depth in our guide to [law firm cash flow management](/blog/law-firm-cash-flow-management). The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) tracks the same idea for hourly practices under the name lockup: the time between doing the work and getting paid for it.

### Capacity opens up without hiring

A case manager who carries 90 files at a 15-month cycle can carry the same client volume with fewer open files at a 12-month cycle. Faster resolution is the cheapest way to relieve the pressure described in our piece on [law firm caseload management](/blog/law-firm-caseload-management).

### Clients stay happier

Clients judge a firm heavily on how long the matter takes and how often they hear about it. A file that moves generates fewer anxious calls, better reviews, and more referrals. A file that sits generates bar complaints.

## Where the Days Go: A Stage-by-Stage Breakdown

Total case cycle time is not actionable. Stage time is. Break every file into stages with a clear start and end event, then measure each one.

A typical pre-litigation **personal injury case timeline** looks like this:

1. **Sign-up to file opened.** Retainer signed, insurance identified, letters of representation sent.
2. **Treatment.** The client is still receiving care. The firm does not control the length, but it does control how closely it monitors progress.
3. **Records and bills collection.** From end of treatment to a complete set of records.
4. **Demand preparation.** From complete records to a demand letter sent to the carrier.
5. **Negotiation.** From demand sent to agreement.
6. **Settlement to disbursement.** Release, lien reduction, check receipt, and payout.

When firms first measure this, the surprise is consistent. Treatment is rarely the worst offender. **The stages the firm fully controls, particularly records collection and demand preparation, are usually where files sit idle.**

Idle time is the key concept. A file in the demand stage for 60 days may have received four hours of actual work. The other 59 days were waiting: for a record nobody chased, for an attorney review that was never scheduled, for a client signature that was requested once and forgotten.

## Five Ways to Shorten Time to Settlement Without Cutting Case Value

Speed has a bad reputation among trial lawyers because it is associated with settling cheap. That is a different thing. The goal is to remove days in which nothing happens, not days in which value is being built.

### 1. Start medical records retrieval before treatment ends

Most firms request records after the client finishes care. Request the early records, such as the emergency room visit and initial imaging, as soon as the file opens, and set a recurring follow-up on every outstanding request. **Medical records retrieval** is the single most common source of dead time in a pre-litigation file, and most of it comes from requests that were sent once and never chased.

### 2. Put a service level on every stage

Each stage needs a target and an owner. For example: letters of representation out within two business days of sign-up, demand package drafted within 21 days of complete records, attorney review within five days of draft. When a file exceeds its target, it should appear on an exception report automatically.

Document these targets the same way you document everything else. Our guide to [law firm standard operating procedures](/blog/law-firm-standard-operating-procedures) explains how to write them so they survive staff turnover.

### 3. Remove the attorney review bottleneck

In many firms, finished demands wait in a partner's inbox. Fix this with a standing weekly review block, a templated **demand package** that makes review fast, and clear authority levels so that routine cases under a set value do not need partner sign-off at all.

### 4. Work liens in parallel, not at the end

Health insurers, Medicare, and medical providers with liens should be identified and put on notice early. Firms that begin lien negotiation only after settlement add weeks to every file at the exact point the client is most impatient.

### 5. Stop feeding slow cases into the pipeline

Some matters are slow because they were never good cases. Disputed liability, thin coverage, and gaps in treatment all predict long, low-value files. Tightening criteria at the front door, as outlined in our guide to [law firm case screening](/blog/law-firm-case-screening), protects case cycle time before the file even opens.

## The Ethics Line: Fast Is Not the Same as Rushed

Diligence is a professional duty, not just a business preference. [ABA Model Rule 1.3](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_3_diligence/) requires reasonable diligence and promptness, and its comments single out procrastination as one of the most resented failings in the profession.

The same duty cuts the other way. Settling before a client reaches maximum medical improvement, or before the full extent of damages is known, trades the client's recovery for the firm's speed. No case cycle time target justifies that.

A practical safeguard is to measure only the stages the firm controls. Treatment length belongs to the client and the doctors. Records, demands, follow-ups, liens, and disbursement belong to you.

Also watch value alongside speed. If average fee per case falls as cycle time falls, the firm is discounting, not improving. The two metrics should be reported together.

## How to Start Measuring Case Cycle Time This Quarter

You do not need new software. Most case management systems already store the dates. What is usually missing is the discipline of recording stage changes consistently.

A workable first 90 days:

- **Weeks 1-2:** Define five to seven stages and the event that starts each one. Make stage updates mandatory.
- **Weeks 3-4:** Pull every case closed in the last 12 months and calculate median days per stage by case type. This is your baseline.
- **Weeks 5-8:** Pick the single worst firm-controlled stage and set a service level for it. Review the exception report weekly.
- **Weeks 9-12:** Compare new stage times against baseline, then move to the next stage.

Fix one stage at a time. Firms that launch six initiatives at once usually finish none of them.

Litigated files need a separate track. Court scheduling sets much of the pace there, and the planning questions are different, as discussed in our analysis of [court backlogs for law firms](/blog/court-backlogs-for-law-firms). Industry research such as the [Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html) reports on law firm financial performance is useful context for how collection speed affects firm health overall.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is case cycle time for a law firm?

Case cycle time is the number of days from a signed retainer to the day the firm receives its fee. It includes every stage in between: treatment, records collection, demand, negotiation, and disbursement. Firms should track it by case type and resolution path rather than as one blended average.

### How long is a typical personal injury case timeline?

It varies widely by injury severity, jurisdiction, and whether suit is filed. Pre-litigation claims commonly resolve within several months to a little over a year after treatment ends, while litigated cases often run two years or longer. The more useful benchmark is your own firm's median by case type, measured stage by stage.

### How can a law firm shorten time to settlement without lowering case value?

Remove idle days, not working days. Request records early and follow up on a schedule, set deadlines for drafting and reviewing demands, and begin lien resolution before settlement. Track average fee per case alongside speed so you can confirm that value is holding.

### Why does medical records retrieval slow cases down so much?

Providers respond slowly, and most firms send a request once and wait. Without a scheduled follow-up, a missing record can stall a file for weeks before anyone notices. Assigning ownership and a recurring follow-up interval to every outstanding request is usually the fastest single improvement a firm can make.

### Does faster case resolution create ethical risk?

It can if speed comes from settling before damages are fully known. Model Rule 1.3 requires promptness, but the client's interests control the decision to settle. Limit cycle time targets to the stages the firm controls and never to the length of a client's medical treatment.
