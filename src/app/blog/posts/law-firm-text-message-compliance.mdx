Most firms worry about law firm text message compliance in one place: the lawsuit. They should also worry about the phone carrier. A text that satisfies every consent rule can still be filtered, throttled, or blocked before the lead ever sees it, and nobody tells your intake team it happened.

That second layer is why firms with clean consent records still report that "texting stopped working." Legal compliance and carrier compliance are two separate systems with two separate enforcers. You need to pass both, or your SMS intake follow-up quietly turns into a dead channel.

## Why Law Firm Text Message Compliance Has Two Layers

The first layer is law. The Telephone Consumer Protection Act treats text messages as calls, which means the same consent, timing, and opt-out rules apply. Statutory damages run **$500 per message, and up to $1,500 if a court finds the violation willful**, which is why class counsel love texting cases.

The second layer is the carrier network. Mobile carriers require businesses sending application-to-person (A2P) texts over standard 10-digit numbers to register their brand and campaign. Unregistered traffic is increasingly filtered or blocked outright.

Here is how the two layers differ:

- **Legal layer:** Enforced by plaintiffs, the FCC, and state attorneys general. Failure means damages and litigation.
- **Carrier layer:** Enforced automatically by filtering and by your texting vendor. Failure means undelivered messages and no warning.

For the legal fundamentals on calls and autodialers, start with our guide to [TCPA compliance for law firms](/blog/tcpa-compliance-law-firms). This article covers what is specific to text.

## Text Message Consent for Leads: What You Actually Need

The core question for any outbound text is whether you hold valid consent for that number, for that kind of message. Informational follow-up to a person who just asked for help is different from marketing, and the consent standard differs accordingly.

For marketing or promotional texts, you need **prior express written consent**. In practice, that means a clear disclosure and an affirmative action by the consumer, tied to your firm specifically.

A defensible text message consent for leads record includes:

1. The exact consent language shown at the time of submission
2. A timestamp, IP address, and the page or form URL
3. The phone number submitted
4. The name of the firm the consumer agreed to hear from
5. A retrievable copy, stored for years, not weeks

Pay attention to the last point. Consent that lives only inside a vendor's dashboard is consent you may not be able to produce when a demand letter arrives.

### The Purchased Lead Problem

Consent attached to a form on a third party's website does not automatically transfer to your firm. In 2025, the Eleventh Circuit vacated the FCC's "one-to-one" consent rule, so the legal picture is less rigid than it was proposed to be. But a consumer who agreed to hear from "marketing partners" generally has not agreed to be texted by your firm in particular.

Ask any lead source to show you the actual consent language and the record for each lead. Our [data broker compliance checklist](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) walks through that diligence step by step.

## A2P 10DLC Registration: The Step Firms Skip

A2P 10DLC registration is the process of registering your business (the "brand") and your texting use case (the "campaign") through your messaging provider, which submits it to The Campaign Registry. Carriers use that information to decide whether your traffic is trusted.

Firms that skip it usually learn about it through symptoms:

- Delivery rates that fall without explanation
- Texts that show as "sent" in the CRM but never arrive
- Messages blocked specifically by one carrier
- Surcharges and fees from the vendor on unregistered traffic

Registration requires accurate business details, a described use case, sample messages, and proof of how people opt in. If your sample messages say one thing and your intake team sends another, carriers can and do suspend campaigns.

The practical lesson: **your registered campaign description is a compliance document.** Write it to match what you actually send, and update it when your process changes.

## Quiet Hours, Opt-Outs, and Message Content

Even with perfect consent and registration, execution can sink you. Three areas generate most of the violations.

### Timing

Federal rules bar telemarketing calls and texts before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in the **recipient's** local time. A firm in New York texting a lead in California at 8:30 a.m. Eastern is already outside the window. Several states, including Florida and Oklahoma, impose stricter limits on their own residents.

### Opt-Outs

The FCC's revocation rules, which took effect in 2025, say consumers can revoke consent by any reasonable means, and businesses must honor requests promptly. "Stop," "quit," "unsubscribe," and plain-English variants like "please don't text me" all count.

Build your opt-out handling around three habits:

1. Treat any clear refusal as a revocation, not only the keyword STOP
2. Sync opt-outs across every system, including CRM, dialer, and email platform
3. Send one confirmation message at most, and then stop completely

### Content

Identify the firm in the first message. Keep case details out of the text. Under ABA Model Rule 1.6, confidentiality duties begin with prospective clients too, and an SMS thread on a shared device can expose far more than you intended. The rules on [prospective client confidentiality](/blog/law-firm-prospective-client-confidentiality) apply directly to texting.

Also remember that lawyer solicitation rules are state-specific. [ABA Model Rule 7.3](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_3_solicitation_of_clients/) restricts certain solicitation, and some states add filing, labeling, or waiting-period requirements for targeted outreach.

## Building a Compliant SMS Intake Follow-Up Workflow

Speed matters. Clio's [Legal Trends research](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has repeatedly shown that firms that respond quickly win more consultations, and text is often the fastest channel. The goal is to keep that speed without taking on risk. Our piece on [speed to lead for law firms](/blog/speed-to-lead-law-firms) explains why minutes matter.

A workable setup looks like this:

1. **Capture consent at the form.** Use clear, firm-specific language and store the record automatically.
2. **Send a single identifying text.** Name the firm, state why you are texting, and include opt-out instructions.
3. **Route replies to a human.** Automation should hand off to staff the moment a lead engages.
4. **Respect the clock.** Queue messages that would land outside the recipient's quiet hours.
5. **Audit monthly.** Sample 25 conversations for consent proof, timing, and opt-out handling.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do law firms need consent to text leads?

Yes, law firms generally need consent before texting a lead, and marketing texts require prior express written consent. Informational replies to someone who just contacted you carry a lower bar, but you should still document the interaction. When in doubt, obtain written consent at the point of form submission.

### What is A2P 10DLC registration and do law firms need it?

A2P 10DLC registration is the carrier-required process of registering your brand and texting campaign for business messages sent from standard local numbers. Law firms sending texts to clients and leads through a business platform need it. Without it, messages are likely to be filtered, blocked, or surcharged.

### What are the TCPA text message rules on quiet hours?

TCPA text message rules prohibit marketing texts before 8 a.m. and after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time zone. Some states set narrower windows. Schedule outbound messages by the lead's time zone, not your office's.

### Can a lead revoke consent by replying with something other than STOP?

Yes, a lead can revoke consent by any reasonable means, including plain-language replies such as "don't text me anymore." Your system should flag and honor these requests, not only the keyword STOP. Log the revocation and suppress the number across every channel.

### How long should we keep text consent records?

Keep consent records for at least four years, since that is the federal statute of limitations for most TCPA claims. Many firms keep them longer to match their [data retention policy](/blog/legal-data-retention-policy). Store the consent language, timestamp, and source with each lead.
