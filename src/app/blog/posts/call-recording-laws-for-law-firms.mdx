A firm in California records every intake call for quality assurance. No beep, no disclosure, no consent script — just a CRM integration that hits record the moment the phone connects. That single habit, repeated across thousands of calls, is a civil claim waiting for a plaintiff's attorney to notice. **Call recording laws for law firms** are not a footnote to TCPA compliance; they're a separate body of state statutes that most intake operations have never actually read.

The irony is hard to miss. Firms that spend cases suing companies over unauthorized recordings and data misuse often run their own intake lines the same way. Recording without disclosure isn't a gray area in twelve states — it's a criminal wiretapping statute with civil damages attached.

## Why Call Recording Laws Are Different From TCPA

Most firms bucket "recording calls" under the same compliance umbrella as [TCPA compliance](/blog/tcpa-compliance-law-firms) — outbound consent, robocall restrictions, do-not-call lists. That's a mistake. TCPA governs *how you contact someone*. Call recording law governs *what you do once you're already talking to them*.

These are separate statutes, separate regulators, and separate causes of action. A firm can be fully TCPA-compliant — proper consent to call, no auto-dialer violations, clean opt-in records — and still violate a state wiretapping law the moment it hits record without telling the person on the other end.

The distinction matters because compliance programs built around TCPA checklists often stop short. They cover consent to contact. They don't cover consent to record.

## The One-Party vs. Two-Party Consent Split

Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 2511) only requires one party to a call to consent to its recording — meaning the firm itself can legally record without telling the caller, under federal law alone. States are where it gets complicated.

Roughly a dozen states require **all** parties to consent before a call can be recorded, commonly called two-party or all-party consent states:

- California
- Florida
- Illinois
- Maryland
- Massachusetts
- Michigan
- Montana
- Nevada (mixed interpretation)
- New Hampshire
- Pennsylvania
- Washington

The rest follow the federal one-party standard, where the firm's own consent to record is legally sufficient. But here's the trap: **the applicable law is often determined by where the caller is located, not where the firm's office sits.** A Texas-based firm (one-party state) fielding an intake call from a prospective client in California is very likely subject to California's all-party consent rule for that specific call.

For firms running national lead generation campaigns across personal injury, mass tort, and medical malpractice, this isn't a hypothetical edge case. It's every single call from a two-party state, which collectively represent close to a quarter of the U.S. population.

## What Violating Call Recording Laws Actually Costs

This isn't a theoretical compliance risk. California's Invasion of Privacy Act (Penal Code § 632) allows for statutory damages of up to $5,000 per violation — per call, not per case. Pennsylvania's Wiretapping and Electronic Surveillance Control Act carries similar exposure, plus the possibility of criminal misdemeanor charges in willful violations. Coverage from the [National Law Review](https://www.natlawreview.com/) has tracked a steady rise in consumer privacy litigation naming businesses that record calls without disclosure, a trend attorneys should recognize since it mirrors claims their own firms have litigated.

Multiply that by an intake team fielding hundreds of calls a week from purchased or organic leads, and the exposure compounds fast. Plaintiff's firms — the same firms often running the campaigns — have increasingly turned this into a plaintiff-side practice area of its own, filing class actions against companies that record without disclosure.

A few numbers worth sitting with:

1. California's $5,000-per-violation statutory damages apply regardless of whether the caller suffered actual harm.
2. Class actions under two-party consent statutes routinely settle in the seven-figure range once aggregated across a call volume-heavy intake operation.
3. Many cyber liability and E&O policies exclude statutory wiretapping claims, meaning the firm's insurance may not cover the exposure at all.

## Building a Compliant Call Recording Law Policy

The fix isn't complicated, but it has to be deliberate. A defensible recording policy for law firms has three components.

### Disclose Before You Record

The safest approach — and the one that sidesteps the one-party/two-party analysis entirely — is to disclose recording on every call, regardless of the caller's state. A short, scripted line at the top of the call ("This call may be recorded for quality assurance") satisfies even the strictest all-party consent statutes, as long as the caller has a genuine opportunity to object or hang up.

This should be built directly into the [intake script](/blog/law-firm-intake-script), not left to the discretion of individual staff. Consistency is what makes the disclosure legally defensible — a policy that's followed 80% of the time is a policy a plaintiff's attorney will use against you. The [ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/) don't address call recording directly, but Rule 1.4's communication obligations reinforce the same principle: clients deserve clear, honest disclosure about how the firm handles their information from the very first call.

### Configure Your Systems to Match Caller Geography

If a blanket disclosure isn't feasible — some firms find it undermines call flow or conversion — the system needs geographic intelligence. That means:

- Identifying the caller's state from the inbound number or CRM record before recording begins
- Automatically triggering a recorded disclosure for calls originating from two-party consent states
- Logging consent (or disclosure delivery) as part of the call record itself, not just the audio file

Most modern [legal CRM](/blog/legal-crm-for-law-firms) platforms and call tracking tools support this kind of conditional logic. If yours doesn't, that's a vendor conversation worth having before your next compliance audit, not after. Intake call volume has only grown as firms invest more heavily in client acquisition — the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has repeatedly shown intake responsiveness as one of the strongest predictors of case conversion, which means recording policy touches nearly every call a growing firm makes.

### Extend the Policy to Outsourced Intake

If your firm uses an [outsourced answering service](/blog/law-firm-answering-service) or a third-party intake vendor, the recording policy has to extend contractually to them. A vendor recording calls on your behalf without proper disclosure creates liability for the firm, not just the vendor — courts have consistently held that the party benefiting from the call (the firm) bears responsibility alongside the party physically recording it.

This is the same due diligence logic that applies to [vetting a lead vendor's compliance posture](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) before buying leads: you inherit the compliance failures of anyone acting on your behalf.

## Where Purchased Leads Add a Layer of Risk

Firms buying leads from third-party vendors face a specific variant of this problem. If the vendor's qualification call — the one that generates the lead in the first place — was recorded without proper disclosure, and that recording is later used in litigation over the lead's authenticity or the client's consent, the firm can be pulled into the underlying wiretapping exposure even though it never touched the recording equipment.

This is a due diligence question worth asking directly: does the vendor disclose recording on qualification calls, and can they produce documentation of it? A vendor that can't answer that question in one sentence hasn't thought about it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do law firms need consent to record intake calls?

It depends on the state the caller is in. In one-party consent states, the firm's own consent is sufficient under federal and most state law. In the roughly dozen all-party consent states — including California, Florida, and Pennsylvania — every participant on the call must be notified and given the opportunity to object before recording begins.

### What happens if a law firm violates call recording laws?

Violations of state wiretapping statutes typically carry statutory civil damages, often $1,000 to $5,000 per violation regardless of actual harm, and in some states can trigger misdemeanor criminal liability for willful violations. Plaintiff's attorneys have increasingly filed class actions against companies with high call volumes and no disclosure practice.

### Is a beep tone enough to satisfy call recording law?

In most jurisdictions, a beep tone alone does not satisfy disclosure requirements — courts generally require an affirmative statement that the call is being recorded, delivered in a way the caller can reasonably hear and understand before recording starts. A verbal disclosure at the start of the call is the more defensible standard.

### Does TCPA compliance cover call recording?

No. TCPA governs consent to contact — outbound calls, texts, and robocalls — while call recording law is a separate body of state wiretapping and privacy statutes governing consent to record a conversation already in progress. A firm can be fully compliant with one and still violate the other.

### Which states require all-party consent to record calls?

The commonly cited all-party consent states include California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, and Washington, with Nevada treated inconsistently depending on the court. Because the caller's location typically governs, firms operating nationally should assume any of these states could apply on a given call.
