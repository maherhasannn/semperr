Most firms treat the law firm conflict check as a formality that happens somewhere between the consultation and the retainer. That worked when new matters arrived by referral, a few at a time, and a partner could clear most of them from memory. It does not work when a firm buys leads, runs ads, and fields 200 inquiries a month through an intake team that has never met half the firm's former clients.

The failure is rarely dramatic at first. It shows up eight months later as a disqualification motion, a fee you have to refund, or a client you must drop in the middle of a case. By then the intake specialist who skipped the search has taken another thousand calls.

## Why the Law Firm Conflict Check Fails at Scale

Conflict checking is a data problem disguised as an ethics problem. The rules have barely changed in decades. What changed is the number of names flowing through the front door and the number of people touching them.

Three things break as volume rises:

- **Memory stops working.** A five-lawyer firm with 300 closed files can rely on recall. A firm with 6,000 closed files and three lateral hires cannot.
- **The search moves later.** Under pressure to respond fast, intake teams book the consultation first and run the search afterward, by which point the firm has already heard confidential facts.
- **The database gets dirty.** Leads enter the CRM with misspelled names, missing adverse parties, and no link to the practice management system where actual clients live.

The second point matters more than most firms realize. Once a prospect shares confidential information in a consultation, the firm owes that person duties even if no retainer is ever signed. We covered that exposure in detail in our piece on [prospective client confidentiality](/blog/law-firm-prospective-client-confidentiality).

Speed and diligence are not actually in tension here. A well-built search takes under two minutes. The firms that skip it are almost always the ones whose process takes twenty.

## The Rules a Conflict of Interest Check Has to Satisfy

Four ABA Model Rules do most of the work. Every state has its own version, and the differences matter, so treat this as a map rather than a substitute for your jurisdiction's text.

### Current clients

[Model Rule 1.7](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_7_conflict_of_interest_current_clients/) bars a representation that is directly adverse to another current client, or where there is a significant risk the representation will be materially limited by duties to someone else or by the lawyer's own interests. Many of these conflicts can be waived with informed consent confirmed in writing. Some cannot.

### Former clients

[Model Rule 1.9](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_9_duties_of_former_clients/) prohibits taking a matter against a former client when it is the same or substantially related to the earlier one, absent written informed consent. This is why closed files stay in the search index forever. A client you last spoke to in 2014 can still conflict you out of a case in 2026.

### Prospective clients

Rule 1.18 extends a lighter version of the former-client duty to people who consulted the firm but never hired it. Prospective client conflicts are the category that grows fastest under paid lead flow, because every unsigned consultation adds a name to the list. A firm that converts 15% of its consultations is creating far more prospective clients than actual ones.

### Imputation

[Model Rule 1.10](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_10_imputation_of_conflicts_of_interest_general_rule/) is the multiplier. With limited exceptions, one lawyer's conflict belongs to every lawyer in the firm. Imputed conflicts are why a lateral hire's old caseload, a new office, or a merger can quietly contaminate matters nobody at the firm thought to check.

## What Your Intake Team Needs to Capture

A search is only as good as the names you feed it. Most intake forms collect the caller's name and phone number and stop there, which makes a real law firm conflict check impossible.

At minimum, capture these before any substantive conversation:

1. **The prospect's full legal name**, plus maiden names, former names, and aliases.
2. **Every adverse party.** The other driver, the employer, the spouse, the landlord, the creditor.
3. **Related entities.** Business names, parent companies, insurers if known, and the owners of closely held businesses.
4. **Other involved people.** Co-defendants, passengers, co-owners, key witnesses, and family members with a stake in the outcome.
5. **Opposing counsel**, if the prospect already knows who it is.
6. **The matter type and a one-line description**, kept deliberately free of confidential detail.

That last item is the discipline that protects you. Train intake staff to gather identities first and facts second. If the search hits, the firm can decline without having learned anything that disqualifies it from the other side.

A script helps. Something as simple as "Before we get into what happened, I need a few names so we can make sure we are able to help you" sets the expectation without sounding bureaucratic. Callers almost never object.

This also fits naturally alongside qualification. The same two minutes that power the search feed your [case screening](/blog/law-firm-case-screening) criteria, so one structured set of questions serves both purposes.

## A Law Firm Conflict Check Workflow That Takes Minutes

The goal is a process fast enough that nobody is tempted to skip it. Here is the sequence that holds up under volume.

### Step 1: Search before the consultation

Run the names as soon as the lead is contacted and the parties are identified, not after the attorney call is booked. For purchased leads, this means the search happens on the first call. It does not conflict with [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-law-firms): you still call in the first five minutes, you just ask for names before facts.

### Step 2: Search everything, not just clients

The index must include current clients, former clients, declined prospects, adverse parties from every past matter, and related entities. A search that only covers signed clients misses the majority of real hits.

### Step 3: Use fuzzy matching

Exact-match searches fail on "Jon Smith" versus "Jonathan Smyth" and on "ABC Holdings LLC" versus "A.B.C. Holdings." A good tool uses phonetic and partial matching and returns possible hits for a human to review. Expect more false positives. That is the system working.

### Step 4: Route hits to a named decision-maker

Intake staff should never clear a hit themselves. Designate one attorney, with a backup, who reviews flagged matches the same day. The reviewer decides among three outcomes: no conflict, conflict waivable with written consent, or decline.

### Step 5: Document the result

Record who ran the search, when, which names were searched, what came back, and who cleared it. If a conflict is ever alleged, a timestamped record showing a reasonable search is the difference between an honest miss and negligence.

### Step 6: Re-run when the facts change

A new defendant is added. An insurer is identified. A co-plaintiff joins. Each new party is a new search. Firms that check once at intake and never again are covered for the case they thought they had, not the case they actually have.

### Where conflict checking software helps and where it does not

Most modern practice management platforms include a conflict search, and standalone tools exist for larger firms. The technology is mature. The gaps are almost always about what data the tool can see.

Ask these questions about the tool behind your law firm conflict check:

- Does the search cover leads and declined prospects in the CRM, or only matters in the practice management system?
- Does it search adverse parties and related contacts, or only client names?
- Does it handle name variants, or only exact strings?
- Does it log every search automatically?
- Can it be run by intake staff without giving them access to confidential matter content?

The first question is where most firms fail. Leads live in one system and clients in another, with no shared index. If you are evaluating a [legal CRM](/blog/legal-crm-for-law-firms), a unified conflict search across both should be on the requirements list.

Adoption of practice technology keeps rising, a pattern the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has tracked for years. But software does not make judgment calls. Whether two matters are "substantially related" under Rule 1.9, or whether a conflict is waivable under Rule 1.7, is a lawyer's decision every time.

## Ethical Screens, Waivers, and Knowing When to Decline

A hit on a law firm conflict check is not automatically a lost case. It is a decision point with three paths.

**Waiver.** Many current-client and former-client conflicts can proceed with informed consent, confirmed in writing, from every affected client. "Informed" has teeth: the client must understand the risks and the alternatives. A boilerplate paragraph buried in the retainer rarely qualifies.

**An ethical screen.** In some situations, a firm can isolate the conflicted lawyer from the matter and avoid imputation. Under the Model Rules this is available for certain lateral-hire conflicts and for prospective client conflicts where the lawyer took reasonable steps to limit what they learned. A real screen means restricted file access, written notice, no fee sharing from the matter, and proof it was in place from the start.

**Decline.** Some conflicts cannot be cured. Representing both sides of the same litigation is the obvious example. When you decline, send a non-engagement letter, say nothing about why that would reveal another client's confidences, and note any deadline the person should know about.

State rules on screening vary considerably. Some jurisdictions are stricter than the Model Rules about when a screen defeats imputation, so confirm your own state's position before relying on one.

## Growth Events That Demand a Fresh Search

The law firm conflict check at routine intake is only half the exposure. The larger conflicts tend to arrive in batches, attached to business decisions nobody thought of as intake.

- **Lateral hires.** A new attorney brings every former client and adverse party with them. Run their prior matters against your database before the offer is final.
- **New practice areas.** Adding employment defense to a plaintiff-side firm, or the reverse, creates positional and client conflicts. This belongs in any [practice area expansion](/blog/law-firm-practice-area-expansion) plan.
- **Mergers and new offices.** Two client lists become one overnight, and imputation follows.
- **Co-counsel and referral arrangements.** Shared matters mean shared parties. Each firm must clear its own conflicts.

In each case the fix is the same: treat the event as a bulk intake and search before the commitment, not after.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a law firm conflict check?

A law firm conflict check is a search of the firm's records to determine whether taking a new matter would conflict with duties owed to a current client, former client, or prospective client. It compares the new client, adverse parties, and related entities against everyone the firm has represented, opposed, or consulted with. The search should be completed before the firm receives confidential information about the matter.

### When should a conflict of interest check be run during intake?

A conflict of interest check should be run before the consultation, as soon as the parties are identified. Running it afterward means the firm may already hold confidential information that creates duties under Rule 1.18, even if it declines the case. The search should be repeated whenever a new party enters the matter.

### Do prospective client conflicts apply if the person never hired the firm?

Yes. Under ABA Model Rule 1.18, a person who consults a firm about possible representation is owed confidentiality, and the firm may be barred from acting against them in the same or a substantially related matter if it received information that could be significantly harmful. This is why declined leads and unsigned consultations must be kept in the conflicts database.

### What should conflict checking software be able to do?

Conflict checking software should search clients, former clients, declined prospects, adverse parties, and related entities in a single query. It should use fuzzy or phonetic matching to catch name variants and automatically log who searched, when, and what was found. It cannot decide whether a hit is a real conflict; that judgment belongs to an attorney.

### Can an ethical screen fix imputed conflicts?

Sometimes. Under the Model Rules, a screen can prevent imputation in specific situations, such as a lateral hire's prior work or a prospective client consultation, if the screen is timely and the affected party receives written notice. State rules differ on when screening is permitted, so firms should verify their own jurisdiction's version before relying on it.
