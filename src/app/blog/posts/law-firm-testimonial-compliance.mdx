Most attorneys treat client reviews as pure upside: collect them, display them, repeat. Law firm testimonial compliance says otherwise. Every review you solicit, edit, filter, or feature is also an advertisement, and advertisements are regulated twice, once by your state bar and once by the Federal Trade Commission.

The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials has been in force since late 2024, and it carries civil penalties above $51,000 per violation. Bar regulators have always policed misleading claims. Firms that built their reputation strategy before either layer tightened are often carrying exposure they have never audited.

## Why Law Firm Testimonial Compliance Is Two Problems, Not One

A testimonial on your website is simultaneously a "communication concerning a lawyer's services" and a commercial endorsement. Different agencies enforce different rules against the same piece of content.

- **State bar rules** govern whether the testimonial is false or misleading, whether it creates unjustified expectations, and whether required disclaimers appear.
- **The FTC** governs how reviews are obtained, whether they are genuine, whether material connections are disclosed, and whether negative reviews are suppressed.

Passing one test does not satisfy the other. A review that is truthful and bar-compliant can still violate FTC rules if you paid for it without disclosure, and a legitimate FTC-clean review can still violate a state rule on result-based claims.

## What the Bar Rules Say About Law Firm Testimonial Compliance

[ABA Model Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/) prohibits false or misleading communications about a lawyer or the lawyer's services. A communication is misleading if it omits a fact necessary to prevent the statement from being materially misleading, or if it is likely to create an unjustified expectation about results.

The model rule does not ban testimonials outright. States, however, have gone their own way, and the variation is wide.

### Common state-level restrictions

- Required disclaimers such as "prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome"
- Bans or limits on testimonials that reference specific case results
- Prohibitions on testimonials from clients in pending matters
- Rules requiring that the firm retain proof of the testimonial's authenticity
- Limits on compensation or incentives tied to reviews

Our guide to [attorney advertising compliance](/blog/attorney-advertising-compliance) covers the broader framework. For testimonials specifically, check your jurisdiction's current advertising rule before you publish anything that mentions an outcome. The ABA's [Model Rule 7.2](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/) on advertising is the reference point most state rules grow from.

## The FTC Rule That Changed Law Firm Testimonial Compliance

The FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials rule, effective October 2024, targets practices that were once routine in legal marketing. It applies to law firms like any other business.

The prohibited conduct includes:

1. **Fake or fabricated reviews**, including reviews written by someone who never used your services
2. **Buying positive reviews**, or offering compensation conditioned on a favorable sentiment
3. **Review suppression**, meaning using threats or intimidation to remove legitimate negative reviews
4. **Insider reviews** from employees or relatives without clear disclosure of the relationship
5. **Fake social media indicators**, such as purchased followers or views used to misrepresent influence

The rule does not stop you from asking happy clients for reviews. It stops you from controlling the outcome. Asking every client for feedback is fine. Asking only the ones who said they were satisfied and routing unhappy clients to a private form can cross into review gating territory, which the FTC views as deceptive.

## How Review Gating and Filtering Create Hidden Liability

Review gating is the most common violation in legal marketing, and many firms do it through software without knowing.

Several reputation tools ask a client to rate their experience first. High scores are routed to Google; low scores are routed to a private feedback form. The result is a public profile that misrepresents actual client sentiment.

Under the FTC's framework, that skews the picture consumers rely on. If your platform does this, turn it off. Send the same review request to every eligible client, regardless of how you expect them to respond.

This also protects your numbers. Firms that see a 4.7 average built on gated reviews are measuring something other than client satisfaction, which undermines the signals discussed in our piece on [law firm online reviews](/blog/law-firm-online-reviews).

## Handling Incentives, Agencies, and Third-Party Reviewers

Incentives are where well-meaning firms get into trouble. A gift card for "leaving a review" is risky in most jurisdictions, and Google's policies prohibit incentivized reviews as well. Even a prize drawing for reviewers can be read as compensation.

If you must offer a thank-you, make it unconditional, equal for all clients, and independent of whether a review is posted or what it says. Better still, skip it.

Agencies add another layer. If a marketing vendor writes, posts, or manages reviews on your behalf, you remain responsible for their conduct. Ask any vendor in writing how reviews are obtained and whether any are paid, incentivized, or scripted.

## A Practical Law Firm Testimonial Compliance Checklist

Use this as an internal audit. Run it against every testimonial currently live on your site, Google Business Profile, and social channels.

1. **Confirm authenticity.** Each testimonial maps to a real client and a real matter, with written consent to publish.
2. **Check for result claims.** Remove or qualify any review that implies a guaranteed or typical outcome.
3. **Add required disclaimers.** Place your state's mandated language near every testimonial, not buried in a footer.
4. **Disclose relationships.** Flag any review from a current or former employee, family member, or referral partner.
5. **Audit your requests.** Verify that every client receives the same review invitation regardless of satisfaction.
6. **Review video testimonials.** Recorded statements get the same scrutiny, and our look at [law firm video marketing](/blog/law-firm-video-marketing) explains why they carry extra weight with prospects.
7. **Keep records.** Store consent forms, request logs, and publication dates for at least as long as your state requires advertising records.
8. **Never edit for sentiment.** Light edits for typos are fine; changing meaning is not.

Responding to negative reviews also needs care. Replying publicly can confirm a client relationship and risk disclosing confidential information, an issue the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) shows firms increasingly face as client expectations for online responsiveness rise.

## Why Compliant Testimonials Still Win Cases

Compliance can look like a cost with no return. The opposite is closer to the truth. Prospective clients trust specific, verifiable reviews, and they discount anything that reads as polished or curated.

A mixed set of authentic reviews, with a few critical ones and thoughtful responses, outperforms a wall of identical five-star praise. That credibility is the basis of the [law firm trust signals](/blog/law-firm-trust-signals) that move a caller to sign before the consultation even starts.

Firms that tighten their process once also gain a reusable system. Standard request language, consent forms, and an audit calendar turn law firm testimonial compliance into routine maintenance rather than a crisis response.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are client testimonials allowed under law firm advertising rules?

Yes, client testimonials are allowed in most jurisdictions, but they must not be false or misleading. Many states require disclaimers, restrict references to specific results, or ban testimonials in certain contexts. Check your state bar's current advertising rule before publishing.

### Does the FTC testimonial rule apply to law firms?

Yes, the FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials rule applies to any business, including law firms. It prohibits fake reviews, purchased reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, and review suppression. Violations can carry civil penalties of more than $51,000 each.

### Can a law firm offer incentives for client reviews?

Generally no, or only with great caution. Incentives conditioned on a positive review are prohibited by the FTC, and many state bars restrict compensation tied to endorsements. If you offer any thank-you, make it unconditional and identical for every client.

### What is review gating and is it a compliance risk for law firms?

Review gating is routing satisfied clients to public review sites while sending unhappy clients to a private form. It is a compliance risk because it misrepresents overall client sentiment, which the FTC treats as deceptive. Send the same review request to every client instead.

### Do law firm testimonial compliance rules apply to Google reviews?

Yes, they apply to any review you solicit, feature, or influence, including Google reviews. You cannot control what clients write, but you can control how you ask and what you amplify. Do not republish reviews that imply guaranteed results without the required disclaimers.
