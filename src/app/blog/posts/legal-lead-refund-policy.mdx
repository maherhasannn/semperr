Most law firms read a vendor's pricing page carefully and its return terms not at all. That is backwards. The **legal lead refund policy** is the clause that decides whether a bad week of leads costs you a rounding error or a five-figure loss.

A vendor can advertise a low price per lead and still be expensive if disconnected numbers, duplicates, and out-of-area callers all count as delivered and billable. The policy is where the real price lives.

## Why a Legal Lead Refund Policy Matters More Than the Price Per Lead

A lead price tells you what a contact costs. The return terms tell you what a contact costs **after** the junk is removed.

Consider two vendors selling at $90 per lead. Vendor A credits any lead with a bad phone number, a duplicate, or the wrong geography. Vendor B credits only leads that were never contacted. If 20% of Vendor B's leads are unusable but still billable, your effective cost per usable lead is $112.50 before you count a single intake hour.

This is why [pay per lead for law firms](/blog/pay-per-lead-for-law-firms) only works when the credit terms are written as carefully as the price. Risk that the vendor does not carry lands on your intake team.

## What Counts as a Returnable Lead

Vague language is the most common problem. "Invalid leads" can mean anything, and in a dispute the vendor decides.

Push for a closed list of returnable categories, defined by observable facts rather than judgment:

- **Invalid contact data:** disconnected number, wrong number, or an email that hard-bounces
- **Duplicates:** the same person delivered to you within a stated window, usually 30 to 90 days
- **Out of scope:** wrong state, wrong practice area, or a caller who is not the injured party or a legal decision-maker
- **Already represented:** the prospect confirms another attorney has the matter
- **Sold twice:** the same lead delivered to another firm despite an exclusivity promise
- **Consent failures:** no verifiable opt-in record for the contact method used

The last two matter most. Shared delivery undermines the whole purpose of [exclusive legal leads](/blog/exclusive-legal-leads), and a lead without provable consent is a compliance liability, not just a wasted fee.

### What vendors will resist

Expect pushback on "already represented" and on "not interested." Vendors treat a prospect who changed their mind as normal funnel loss, and that is fair. A prospect who denies ever submitting a form is different, and your agreement should say so.

## The Clauses That Separate Real Policies From Marketing Copy

A refund policy is only as good as the mechanics around it. Check each of these before you sign.

1. **Return window.** Seventy-two hours is common and workable only if your team calls within minutes. Five to seven days is better for phone-verified leads.
2. **Credit versus cash.** Many vendors offer account credit only. Credit is fine if you keep buying, but it traps money if you leave. Ask for the cash-out terms.
3. **Burden of proof.** Specify what evidence closes a dispute: a call log, a recording, or a screenshot. Without this, the vendor can reject returns arbitrarily.
4. **Response deadline.** The vendor should approve or deny within a fixed number of business days, and silence should default to approval.
5. **Return caps.** Some contracts limit returns to a percentage of monthly volume. A cap of 5% is not a refund policy when the vendor's actual bad-lead rate is 15%.
6. **Replacement versus refund.** Replacement leads are only useful if the vendor can deliver them on time. In a seasonal spike, a replacement a week later may be worthless.

If a vendor will not put these in writing, assume they will not honor them informally.

## Compliance Lives Inside the Refund Conversation

Refund terms and compliance terms are connected more than most firms realize. A lead that arrived without valid consent is not merely returnable; it may be unusable.

Under the TCPA, calling or texting a prospect without proper prior express written consent can create statutory damages per violation. Our guide to [TCPA compliance for law firms](/blog/tcpa-compliance-law-firms) covers what valid consent must contain. Your return policy should treat missing or unverifiable consent as a full-credit event, and the vendor should hand over the consent record on request.

The same applies to ethics rules. The American Bar Association's [Model Rule 7.3 on solicitation](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_3_solicitation_of_clients/) restricts live person-to-person contact with prospects, and state versions vary. If a vendor's lead flow could put your firm on the wrong side of those rules, no credit makes up for the exposure. Run your vendor through the checks in [lead generation rules for attorneys](/blog/lead-generation-rules-for-attorneys) before negotiating anything else.

## How to Document and Enforce Returns

A strong clause does nothing if your team never invokes it. Most firms leave credits unclaimed because returns are tedious and nobody owns them.

Build a lightweight process:

1. **Log every contact attempt** in your CRM with timestamps, so a "never answered" claim is provable.
2. **Tag disposition codes** at first touch: wrong number, duplicate, out of area, already represented.
3. **Batch returns weekly** in the format the vendor requires, rather than one at a time.
4. **Track credit rate by vendor and source.** A vendor returning 25% of volume is telling you something about their traffic.
5. **Review monthly** against signed cases, not just against leads delivered.

The Clio [Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) consistently shows that prospects contact multiple firms and favor the first to respond. A return process that slows your calls to verify leads defeats itself, so contact first and document in parallel.

## Return Rates Are a Quality Signal, Not Just a Cost Item

Your credit rate is useful data. A healthy source usually returns a modest share of leads; a source with a persistent high return rate is selling volume without qualification.

Pair return data with downstream results. Track contact rate, consultation rate, and signed-case rate by source, and use a framework like [law firm lead scoring](/blog/law-firm-lead-scoring) to see whether a vendor's accepted leads actually convert. A vendor with low returns and low signings is passing bad leads that do not trip the return rules, which means your definition of "bad" is too narrow.

When you evaluate vendors, apply the same scrutiny covered in [legal lead generation companies](/blog/legal-lead-generation-companies): ask for references, sample data, and the vendor's own historical return rate. A vendor confident in lead quality will share it.

## Beyond Refunds: Buying Leads That Rarely Need Returning

The best refund policy is one you rarely use. Return mechanics reduce losses; they do not create cases.

The stronger lever is upstream. [High-intent legal leads](/blog/high-intent-legal-leads) come from prospects who have taken a deliberate action, such as calling about a specific incident, so contact data is accurate and fit is clear. Fewer of those leads are duplicates, disconnected, or out of scope in the first place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a legal lead refund policy?

A legal lead refund policy is the part of a vendor agreement that defines which delivered leads you can return for credit or cash and how. It should specify the return window, the qualifying reasons, the evidence required, and the vendor's response deadline. Without those four elements, the policy is a marketing statement rather than an enforceable term.

### Can you get a refund on bad legal leads?

Yes, if your contract provides for it. Most vendors credit invalid contact data, duplicates, and out-of-area leads within a short window. Refunds are rarely available for prospects who simply decline to hire you, so read the exact categories before you buy.

### How long is a typical return window for legal leads?

Typical return windows run from 48 hours to seven days from delivery. Shorter windows favor the vendor because your team may not have had time to reach every prospect. Negotiate for at least five days on phone-verified leads and require the clock to start at delivery, not at purchase.

### Is account credit better than a cash refund for bad leads?

Cash is better for your cash flow, while credit is easier to get. Credit locks money inside one vendor relationship, which weakens your leverage if quality drops. Ask for cash-out rights on unused balances so you can leave without losing funds.

### What should a law firm do if a lead vendor refuses valid returns?

Start with your documentation: call logs, timestamps, and consent records. Escalate in writing citing the contract clause, and pause purchases until the dispute resolves. If the vendor continues refusing valid returns, treat that as a signal to move your budget elsewhere.
