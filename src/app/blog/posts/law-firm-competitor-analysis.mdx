Ask a managing partner who the firm competes with and you will get three or four names, usually the firms with the biggest billboards or the ones that have poached staff. Ask a prospect who just searched for a lawyer on their phone and you will get a different list entirely. A **law firm competitor analysis** exists to close that gap, and most firms have never run one.

The cost of skipping it is not abstract. Firms bid on the same keywords as rivals with ten times the budget, copy the positioning of whoever is loudest, and never notice the obvious openings: the competitor who does not answer the phone after five, the one with no Spanish-language intake, the one whose reviews all complain about the same thing.

## What a Law Firm Competitor Analysis Is Actually For

The point is not a slide deck comparing logos. A law firm competitor analysis should end in a short list of decisions.

A useful analysis answers three questions. Where are you spending money to fight a battle you cannot win? Where is a rival weak in a way prospects can feel? And what are you doing that nobody else in the market does, whether or not you are saying so?

That makes it different from general market research. You are not trying to describe the market. You are trying to find the two or three places where a modest reallocation of budget or effort produces a disproportionate number of signed cases.

It also needs a schedule. Local legal markets move fast: a private-equity-backed entrant, a new office from a regional advertiser, or a rival's fresh ad campaign can change your acquisition costs within a quarter. A full review once a year with a lighter check every quarter is a reasonable rhythm for most practices.

## Step One: Identify Who You Really Compete With

Your competitors are whoever shows up when your prospect goes looking. That set changes by channel, so build the list channel by channel rather than from memory.

Run the searches a prospect would run, from the locations your clients actually live in, and record who appears in each of these places:

- **The local map pack.** The three firms shown for "car accident lawyer near me" in a given zip code are your competitors for that zip code, regardless of their size.
- **Paid search and Local Services Ads.** Note who holds the top positions and at what times of day.
- **Organic results.** Include directories and aggregators, since they compete for the same click even though they are not firms.
- **AI-generated answers.** Ask the major AI assistants to recommend a lawyer for your practice area and city, and write down who gets named.
- **Offline media.** Billboards, radio, television, and transit in your market, noted by simple observation.
- **Referral sources.** Ask your best referring attorneys who else they send work to.

You will end up with three tiers. **Direct competitors** want the same cases in the same geography. **Budget competitors** are the heavy advertisers who set auction prices whether or not they win the cases you want. **Indirect competitors** are directories, out-of-market firms buying your keywords, and referral networks.

Most firms only track the second tier, because those are the names on the billboards. The first tier is where the cases are actually won and lost.

## Step Two: Audit What Competitors Are Doing

Limit the deep audit portion of your law firm competitor analysis to five to eight firms. More than that and the work never gets finished.

### Search visibility and keywords

Start with what each rival ranks for and what they pay for. Standard SEO tools will estimate organic keywords, traffic by page, and backlink sources for any domain. Treat the numbers as directional, not precise.

The value of **competitor keyword research** is in the pattern, not the totals. Look for practice-area pages and local pages that rank for a rival and do not exist on your site. Look for terms where every firm on page one has thin content. Those are the openings a focused [law firm SEO strategy](/blog/law-firm-seo-strategy) can take in months rather than years.

On the paid side, Google's Ads Transparency Center and Meta's Ad Library both show the ads a given advertiser is running, free of charge. Read the copy and the offers. If five firms all lead with "no fee unless we win," that line has stopped differentiating anyone.

### Local presence and reviews

For each competitor, record Google Business Profile categories, review count, average rating, review velocity over the last ninety days, and whether the firm responds to reviews. Review velocity matters more than the total; a firm adding fifteen reviews a month is investing, while one sitting on an old pile is coasting.

Then read the negative reviews. This is the cheapest **law firm competitive intelligence** available, because unhappy clients tell you exactly where a rival fails. The complaints cluster: nobody called back, the case was handed to a paralegal, the client never knew what was happening.

Each cluster is a positioning opportunity, provided your own firm does not have the same problem. Check your own [online reviews](/blog/law-firm-online-reviews) with the same honesty before you build a message on it.

### Intake and responsiveness

This is the step firms skip, and it is the one with the most signal. Contact each competitor the way a prospect would: a call during business hours, a call after hours, a web form, and a chat message if they offer one.

Record what happened. Did a person answer? How long until a callback? Did anyone follow up a second time? Was a consultation offered on the first contact?

The results are usually worse than firms expect. The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has repeatedly run secret-shopper studies of hundreds of firms and found that a large share never respond to phone or email inquiries at all. If the dominant advertiser in your market is slow to respond, [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-law-firms) is a gap you can exploit without outspending anyone.

A note on ethics: keep these inquiries brief, do not invent an elaborate fake case that consumes a rival's attorney time, and do not solicit confidential information. You are testing response, not extracting advice.

### Positioning and offer

Finally, read each firm's homepage, main practice pages, and ads, and write one sentence describing what they claim to be. Most will reduce to the same sentence: experienced, aggressive, and caring, with free consultations.

Note anything concrete: a stated response-time promise, a narrow case-type focus, published results, bilingual staff, evening hours, a fee structure that differs from the norm. Concrete claims are rare, which is exactly why they work.

## Step Three: Turn Competitive Analysis Into a Gap Map

Raw notes from a law firm competitor analysis are not useful until they are compared. Build a simple grid: competitors across the top, dimensions down the side, and a rating in each cell.

The dimensions that matter for most consumer practices:

1. Map pack visibility in your priority zip codes
2. Organic rankings for your top practice-area terms
3. Paid search presence and estimated intensity
4. Review count, rating, and velocity
5. Response time across phone, form, and chat
6. After-hours and weekend coverage
7. Language coverage
8. Clarity and specificity of positioning
9. Offline media presence

Add your own firm as a column and rate it with the same rigor. Then look for two patterns.

The first is **crowded rows**, where every firm is strong. Competing there means paying the highest prices in the market for marginal gains. The broad, high-volume personal injury keywords usually fall into this category, which is a large part of why [legal advertising costs](/blog/legal-advertising-costs) keep climbing.

The second is **empty rows**, where nobody is strong. Those are the gaps. A market where no firm answers after 6 p.m., no firm has real Spanish-language intake, or no firm owns a specific case type is a market with room in it.

This is also where **competitive analysis for law firms** tends to disappoint people who wanted a secret. The gaps are rarely exotic. They are usually operational weaknesses that are visible to any prospect and ignored by every firm.

## Step Four: Estimate Law Firm Market Share Honestly

You cannot measure **law firm market share** precisely, because nobody publishes signed-case counts. You can approximate it well enough to make decisions.

Three proxies work together:

- **Share of search visibility.** For a fixed list of twenty to thirty keywords, the percentage of top-three map and organic positions each firm holds.
- **Share of reviews.** Each firm's portion of new reviews across your competitor set over the past twelve months, which tracks roughly with client volume.
- **Share of filings.** In practice areas where cases get filed, court docket data shows which firms appear as counsel of record and how often.

None of these is accurate alone. Together they tell you whether you are a top-three firm in your market or a distant eighth, and that answer should change your strategy. A top-three firm defends and widens its lead. A distant eighth should not try to win the whole metro; it should pick a neighborhood, a case type, or a client segment and dominate it.

Track the same proxies each quarter. The trend matters more than any single reading.

## What to Do With the Findings

A law firm competitor analysis that does not change a budget line or a process was a waste of the week. Translate each finding into one of four moves.

**Stop.** Pull spend from the crowded rows where you are a minor player. A firm with a modest budget bidding on the most expensive head terms against regional advertisers is funding the auction, not winning it. Redirect that money to the narrower terms where your paid search can hold a top position.

**Attack.** Pick one or two empty rows and commit. If nobody answers after hours, staff it and say so in every ad. If a rival's reviews are full of communication complaints, build a specific, provable communication promise.

**Differentiate.** Rewrite your positioning around what the grid shows is actually distinct. Real **law firm differentiation** comes from a claim competitors cannot or will not match, which is the core of any durable [branding strategy](/blog/law-firm-branding-strategy).

**Defend.** Identify where you are strong and a rival is closing in, such as a competitor whose review velocity now exceeds yours, and respond before the gap closes.

One caution on messaging. Comparative claims in attorney advertising are regulated. Under [ABA Model Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communications_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/), an unsubstantiated comparison of your services or fees with those of other lawyers can be misleading. Use what you learn to shape what you say about yourself, and state it in terms you can prove, rather than naming or disparaging a competitor.

## Common Mistakes in Law Firm Competitor Analysis

A few errors show up repeatedly.

- **Copying the market leader.** The biggest advertiser's tactics work because of their budget and brand recognition. The same tactics at one-tenth the spend usually fail.
- **Auditing only marketing.** Intake, hours, and responsiveness decide more cases than ad copy does, and they are the easiest gaps to find.
- **Trusting tool estimates as fact.** Third-party traffic and ad-spend figures are modeled. Use them to rank competitors against each other, not as real numbers.
- **Running it once.** A two-year-old analysis describes a market that no longer exists.
- **Ignoring your own column.** Firms rate rivals harshly and themselves generously. Have someone outside the marketing team run the secret-shopper test on your own intake.

The broader industry data supports paying attention. Research from the [Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html) on the legal market has consistently documented rising competition for demand and growing pressure on firms to differentiate. Firms that know exactly where they stand make better use of every dollar than firms that guess.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a law firm competitor analysis?

A law firm competitor analysis is a structured review of the firms competing for the same cases in the same market. It covers their search visibility, advertising, reviews, intake responsiveness, and positioning. The goal is to identify where rivals are weak and where your own spend is being wasted, so you can reallocate budget and effort toward gaps you can win.

### How often should a law firm run a competitive analysis?

Most firms should run a full competitive analysis once a year and a lighter review every quarter. The quarterly check should cover map pack rankings, review velocity, and active ads, since those change fastest. Run an additional review whenever a major new advertiser enters your market or your cost per case shifts sharply.

### What tools are used for competitor keyword research for law firms?

This kind of research typically relies on a standard SEO platform for organic rankings and backlink data, plus Google's Ads Transparency Center and Meta's Ad Library for active ads. Manual searches from your target zip codes remain essential, because local results vary by location. Treat all third-party traffic and spend estimates as directional rather than exact.

### Is it ethical to secret-shop a competing law firm's intake?

Testing a competitor's response time as a prospective caller is a common practice, but it should be kept brief and honest in scope. Do not fabricate a detailed case that consumes attorney time, and do not seek confidential information or legal advice under false pretenses. Check your state's rules of professional conduct, particularly those on dishonesty and misrepresentation, before designing the test.

### How do you measure law firm market share in a local market?

It cannot be measured exactly, but it can be estimated with three proxies: share of top search positions across a fixed keyword list, share of new reviews over the past year, and share of court filings in your practice area. Used together and tracked quarterly, these show your relative position and whether it is improving.
