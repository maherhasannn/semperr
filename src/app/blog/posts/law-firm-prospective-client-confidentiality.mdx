Most attorneys think confidentiality begins when the engagement letter is signed. It does not. Prospective client confidentiality attaches the moment a stranger describes their problem to your intake coordinator, your chatbot, or a web form, whether or not you ever take the case.

That matters because intake is where firms collect the most sensitive facts with the least process. Injury details, immigration status, criminal history, and marital secrets flow into CRMs, call recordings, and third-party lead vendors, often with no thought about who else can see them.

## What Rule 1.18 Actually Requires

ABA Model Rule 1.18 defines a prospective client as a person who consults with a lawyer about the possibility of forming a client-lawyer relationship. The full text is available from the [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_18_duties_to_prospective_client/), and most states have adopted a version of it.

The rule creates three practical duties:

- **Confidentiality.** You cannot use or reveal information learned during the consultation, even if no representation follows.
- **Conflict exposure.** If you received information that could be significantly harmful to the prospective client, you may be barred from representing an adverse party in the same matter.
- **Imputation.** That bar generally extends to every lawyer in the firm unless you meet the screening exception.

The screening exception is narrow. You must have limited the information you took in to what was reasonably necessary, wall off the lawyer who received it, and give prompt written notice to the prospective client.

## Why Intake Is the Highest-Risk Point for Prospective Client Confidentiality

A lawyer who consults for 20 minutes in a conference room is careful by instinct. An intake team handling 60 calls a day is not, because the training, scripts, and software were built for speed.

Three habits create most of the exposure:

1. **Over-collecting facts.** Intake staff who ask for full narratives before a conflict check can poison the firm against future clients on the other side.
2. **Skipping the conflict check.** Many firms run conflicts only after a case is accepted, which defeats the point of the rule.
3. **Open access.** Every prospect record, including declined ones, sits in a CRM visible to staff with no need to see it.

A tighter [law firm intake script](/blog/law-firm-intake-script) fixes the first problem. Collect identifying information and the adverse party first, run the conflict check, and only then gather case facts.

## Where Prospect Data Leaks

Confidentiality failures rarely come from a single dramatic breach. They come from ordinary tools configured for convenience.

### Web forms and chat widgets

Contact forms usually email submissions to a shared inbox. Chat widgets store transcripts with a third-party vendor. If a visitor types case details into either one, you have taken on a duty to protect that information.

Add a clear disclaimer near every form field stating that submitting information does not create an attorney-client relationship and that the firm cannot guarantee confidentiality of unsolicited messages. ABA Formal Opinion 10-457 recognizes that well-drafted disclaimers can limit a visitor's reasonable expectation of confidentiality. A disclaimer helps, but it will not save you if your own form invited detailed facts. The same tracking concerns discussed in our guide to [CIPA compliance for law firms](/blog/cipa-compliance-law-firms) apply to what those widgets capture.

### Call recording and voicemail

Recorded intake calls contain the full narrative in the prospect's own words. Consent rules vary by state, and retention is a separate question. Review the [call recording consent rules](/blog/law-firm-call-recording-compliance) before you decide how long recordings live and who can replay them.

### Marketing and lead vendors

This is the leak most firms miss. When a vendor sells the same inquiry to several firms, a prospect's story reaches multiple lawyers at once. That raises obvious questions about who consulted whom, and whether a conflict now exists across those firms.

It is one more reason [exclusive leads](/blog/exclusive-legal-leads) carry lower compliance risk than shared ones. Before you buy from anyone, work through the [data broker due diligence checklist](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) and ask exactly where inquiry data is stored and sold.

## Building a Prospect Confidentiality Protocol

You do not need a large compliance budget. You need a short written protocol that intake staff can follow without thinking.

A workable version includes:

- **Limited-information intake.** Staff gather name, contact details, adverse parties, and a one-line case type before any narrative.
- **Conflict check before facts.** Nothing substantive is recorded until the check clears.
- **Role-based CRM access.** Only intake, the screening attorney, and the assigned lawyer can open prospect notes.
- **Non-engagement letters.** Every declined prospect gets a written notice that the firm is not representing them, along with statute of limitations warnings where appropriate.
- **Defined retention.** Declined-prospect records are purged or archived on a set schedule, consistent with your [data retention policy](/blog/legal-data-retention-policy).

Train on the protocol quarterly and keep a signed acknowledgment from every new intake hire.

## Screening Procedures When You Have Already Heard Too Much

Sometimes the problem surfaces after the fact. A prospect described a dispute in detail, you declined, and now the opposing party calls.

Act in this order:

1. Identify exactly who at the firm received the information and what they heard.
2. Determine whether the information could be significantly harmful to the prospect in the matter.
3. If it could, build the screen immediately: block CRM access, document the wall, and bar the exposed lawyer from the matter and from fee sharing.
4. Send prompt written notice to the former prospect.
5. Record every step with dates, because you may need to prove the timing to a disciplinary board or court.

If the information was not significantly harmful, document that analysis too. A contemporaneous memo is far stronger than a reconstruction months later.

## Making Your Intake Stack Defensible

Technology can help or hurt. Automation that collects structured fields up front reduces the narrative volume sitting in free-text notes, which supports the limited-information approach. Our overview of [legal intake automation](/blog/legal-intake-automation) covers how to design those workflows.

Whatever tools you choose, apply the safeguards in Model Rule 1.6, the [duty of confidentiality](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_6_confidentiality_of_information/), to prospect data as well. That means encryption, access controls, and vendor agreements that prohibit secondary use of intake information. The [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) consistently shows how many firms still lose inquiries to slow response, so speed matters, but never at the cost of a documented process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does prospective client confidentiality apply if I never take the case?

Yes. Under Model Rule 1.18 and its state equivalents, you owe a prospective client confidentiality even when no representation follows. You generally cannot use or reveal what you learned, and you may be disqualified from representing an adverse party in the same matter.

### Who counts as a prospective client under Rule 1.18?

A prospective client is anyone who consults with a lawyer about possibly forming a client-lawyer relationship. Courts generally look at whether the person reasonably believed they were seeking legal help, so a detailed web form or intake call can qualify. A person who sends unsolicited information after reading a clear disclaimer is less likely to qualify.

### Can a law firm screen a lawyer to avoid imputed conflicts from a prospective client?

Yes, if the firm meets the screening exception in Rule 1.18(d). The firm must have limited the information it received, timely screen the exposed lawyer from the matter, and give prompt written notice to the prospective client. Some states restrict or modify this exception, so check your jurisdiction's version of the rule.

### Does a website disclaimer protect my law firm from prospective client confidentiality duties?

A disclaimer helps but does not eliminate the duty. It can limit a visitor's reasonable expectation of confidentiality, as discussed in ABA Formal Opinion 10-457, but only if it is prominent and the form does not invite detailed case facts. Treat it as one layer alongside limited-information intake and conflict checks.
