Most law firms running paid search have never touched **Local Services Ads for law firms**, and that's a mistake worth correcting. While everyone fights over the same Google Ads keywords, LSAs sit in a separate auction, above the traditional paid results, and bill on a completely different model: you pay per lead, not per click.

That distinction changes the math on customer acquisition entirely. A firm can burn through a five-figure PPC budget on clicks that never convert. With LSAs, every dollar spent is tied to an actual inbound contact — a phone call or message from someone who found the firm through Google's "Google Screened" badge program.

## What Local Services Ads Actually Are

Local Services Ads (LSAs) are Google's verified local advertising product, originally built for home services and expanded to legal, financial, and other professional categories. Firms that enroll get a **Google Screened** badge after passing a background check and license verification, and their listing appears at the very top of relevant local search results — above both organic listings and standard PPC ads.

Unlike [law firm PPC advertising](/blog/law-firm-ppc-advertising), which charges per click regardless of outcome, LSAs charge per qualified lead. A "lead" is a phone call of sufficient duration or a message through the platform. If someone clicks your ad and doesn't contact you, you pay nothing.

This is the core appeal: LSAs convert budget into leads by design, not by hope. That said, "lead" doesn't mean "case." A qualified LSA lead still has to be intake-screened, and firms that skip that step end up paying for calls that were never going to sign.

## How Google Local Services Ads Pricing Works

Pricing for LSAs runs on a bid-based, pay-per-lead model that varies heavily by practice area and market. Personal injury and other high-value practice areas see some of the highest per-lead costs in the entire legal advertising ecosystem, consistent with the rising client acquisition costs documented in the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/).

Rough per-lead cost ranges reported by firms actively running LSA campaigns:

- **Personal injury**: $150–$400+ per lead in competitive metros
- **Family law and divorce**: $50–$150 per lead
- **Criminal defense**: $40–$120 per lead
- **Estate planning**: $30–$90 per lead
- **Immigration**: $40–$100 per lead

These ranges move constantly based on local competition, your review volume, response speed, and your weekly budget cap. Firms new to the platform typically see higher costs per lead in the first few weeks while Google's algorithm calibrates their ranking against review count, proximity, and responsiveness.

Compare this to blended [cost per case](/blog/law-firm-cost-per-case) figures across channels — LSA leads often come in cheaper per contact than PPC, but the qualification rate needs scrutiny before you can call it cheaper per signed case.

## Local Services Ads vs. Traditional PPC for Law Firms

The comparison firms actually need to make isn't LSA versus nothing — it's LSA versus the PPC budget they're already running. Both channels serve high-intent searchers, but the mechanics diverge sharply.

**LSAs:**
- Pay per lead (call or message), not per click
- Ranking driven by reviews, proximity, and responsiveness — not just bid size
- Requires Google Screened background check and licensing verification
- Leads can be disputed and refunded if invalid (wrong practice area, spam, out-of-service-area)
- Limited ad copy control — Google controls the listing format

**Traditional Google Ads (PPC):**
- Pay per click regardless of conversion
- Full control over keywords, ad copy, landing pages, and bid strategy
- No review-score dependency to rank, though ad quality score still matters
- Retargeting and remarketing available; LSAs offer none
- Requires more hands-on optimization and a dedicated landing page strategy

Most firms with real budget run both concurrently. LSAs capture bottom-of-funnel, "I need a lawyer right now" searchers, while PPC and organic search build the broader visibility layer LSAs can't reach on their own.

## The Google Screened Badge Requirement

Getting the Google Screened badge isn't automatic. Google runs a background check on the firm and, in most states, on individual attorneys named on the account. This includes license verification, insurance confirmation in some categories, and a business registration check.

The process typically takes one to three weeks depending on the state and how quickly documentation is submitted. Firms with multiple attorneys or multiple office locations should expect a longer verification cycle, since each location and named attorney may need separate confirmation. Firms should also confirm their listing and ad copy meet state bar advertising rules, which the [ABA Legal Technology Resource Center](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/legal_technology_resources/) tracks alongside broader legal marketing compliance guidance.

A few practical notes for firms going through screening:

1. Have your bar license number, malpractice insurance certificate, and business registration ready before starting the application — incomplete submissions are the most common cause of delay.
2. List every attorney who will field LSA calls, not just the managing partner, since Google may screen individuals separately.
3. Budget for a soft-launch period once approved — ranking takes time to stabilize as Google gathers performance signals.

## Managing Lead Quality and Dispute Rates

The single biggest complaint firms have about LSAs is lead quality — calls from the wrong practice area, spam numbers, or callers outside the service radius. Google allows disputes on leads that meet specific invalid criteria, and firms that don't actively dispute bad leads are effectively donating budget back to Google.

Dispute categories that typically qualify for a refund include wrong practice area, duplicate leads, spam or solicitation calls, and calls that disconnect before any conversation occurs. Google's dispute window is limited, so leads need to be reviewed within days, not weeks — which means whoever answers your phones needs a fast, consistent process for flagging bad leads immediately.

This is where [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-law-firms) discipline pays off twice: fast response improves your LSA ranking (Google factors in responsiveness), and fast triage catches disputable leads before the window closes. Firms without a dedicated intake process tend to under-dispute by a wide margin, quietly eating costs they didn't need to absorb.

## Reviews Are the Real Ranking Currency

Unlike PPC, where bid amount is the dominant ranking lever, LSA ranking weighs review volume and rating heavily alongside bid. A firm with 150 five-star reviews and a modest budget can consistently outrank a competitor spending three times as much with 20 reviews — a pattern consistent with client-trust research tracked by the [Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html).

This makes LSAs a strange hybrid: a paid channel that behaves like local SEO in disguise. Firms serious about LSA performance need an active review-generation process built into their [client intake](/blog/legal-client-intake) workflow — asking for reviews at the moment of case resolution, not as an afterthought months later.

Firms evaluating any paid lead source, LSAs included, should also weigh channel performance against [broader lead generation benchmarks](/blog/legal-lead-generation-benchmarks) to know whether a given cost per lead is actually competitive for their practice area and market.

## Is It Worth It for Your Practice Area?

LSAs work best for practice areas with clear urgency and local intent: personal injury, criminal defense, family law, DUI, and immigration all see strong performance because searchers are looking to hire quickly and locally. Practice areas with longer sales cycles — complex commercial litigation, estate planning for high-net-worth clients — see weaker returns, since those searches rarely convert on a first call.

Firms deciding whether to allocate budget here should run a controlled test: a fixed weekly cap over 60–90 days, tracked against signed cases, not just lead count. That window is usually enough for Google's ranking algorithm to stabilize and for a firm to see a true blended cost per case rather than an early-launch anomaly.

Whether LSAs replace, supplement, or sit alongside your existing paid channels, the underlying principle doesn't change: a lead is only worth what it converts into. Firms that pair a strong paid lead source with disciplined intake and fast follow-up consistently outperform firms chasing the cheapest cost-per-lead number on the page.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are Local Services Ads for law firms?

LSAs are Google's pay-per-lead advertising product that places verified, "Google Screened" law firms at the top of local search results. Firms pay only for qualifying leads — phone calls or messages — rather than paying per click like traditional PPC.

### How much do Local Services Ads cost per lead for attorneys?

Cost per lead varies widely by practice area and market, typically ranging from $30–$90 for estate planning up to $150–$400+ for personal injury in competitive metros. Pricing is bid-based and also influenced by your review volume, proximity to the searcher, and response speed.

### How is Google Local Services Ads pricing different from PPC?

LSA pricing charges per qualified lead (a call or message), while traditional PPC charges per click regardless of whether the click converts to contact. LSAs also rank primarily on reviews, proximity, and responsiveness rather than bid size alone.

### Can law firms dispute bad LSA leads?

Yes. Google allows disputes for leads that are the wrong practice area, duplicates, spam, or disconnected calls, and approved disputes are refunded. The dispute window is short, so leads need review within days of coming in, not weeks.

### How long does it take to get the Google Screened badge?

Verification typically takes one to three weeks, depending on the state and how quickly a firm submits license, insurance, and business registration documentation. Firms with multiple attorneys or offices should expect a longer screening cycle.
