Most firms hire a **law firm marketing agency** the same way they hire an associate: on a good interview and a gut feeling. Six months later, the invoices are consistent but the caseload isn't, and nobody on either side can point to a number that explains why. The agency relationship fails less often because of bad creative and more often because the firm never defined what it was buying.

That distinction matters because legal marketing spend is no longer a rounding error. The average firm now spends between 2% and 15% of gross revenue on marketing, according to the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/), with plaintiff-side and consumer-facing practices at the high end of that range. When six or seven figures are on the line, "they seemed like they knew what they were doing" isn't a vetting process.

## What a Law Firm Marketing Agency Actually Does

A **law firm marketing agency** is not one thing. Under that label you'll find full-service shops running SEO, PPC, and content together; media buyers who only touch paid spend; and boutique consultancies that build strategy but hand off execution to subcontractors. Knowing which type you're evaluating changes every question that follows.

Full-service agencies typically bundle:

- Search engine optimization and technical site work
- Paid search and paid social management
- Content production and [content marketing](/blog/legal-content-marketing)
- Conversion tracking and reporting
- Occasionally, intake and CRM consulting

Specialist shops do one of those well and refer out the rest. Neither model is wrong, but a firm that hires a PPC specialist expecting brand strategy — or a branding shop expecting granular attribution — is setting up a mismatch that no amount of good communication fixes.

## Pricing Models and What They Actually Cost

Legal marketing agency pricing generally falls into four structures, and each one shifts incentives in a different direction.

1. **Flat retainer** — a fixed monthly fee regardless of spend or output. Predictable, but easy for an agency to under-resource once the contract is signed.
2. **Percentage of ad spend** — typically 10-20% of managed media budget. This scales with your investment but can quietly incentivize the agency to recommend more spend rather than better spend.
3. **Performance-based** — fees tied to leads, signed cases, or cost per case. Attractive on paper, but almost no reputable agency will accept full performance risk on case value they don't control end-to-end.
4. **Hybrid** — a smaller base retainer plus a percentage of spend or a bonus tied to agreed KPIs.

For a mid-sized firm running active SEO and paid campaigns, all-in agency costs commonly land between $8,000 and $30,000 per month, separate from ad spend itself. That range should sit inside the broader [marketing budget](/blog/law-firm-marketing-budget) you've already set for the year — if it doesn't, the agency conversation is happening before the budget conversation, which is backwards.

## In-House Marketing vs Agency: The Real Trade-Off

The in-house marketing vs agency decision isn't really about cost — a senior in-house marketer plus specialists can cost more than a mid-tier agency. It's about which gaps you're willing to live with.

**In-house teams win on:**
- Institutional knowledge of your practice areas and cases
- Faster internal turnaround and no vendor lag
- Direct accountability without a contract to renegotiate

**Agencies win on:**
- Access to a full bench (SEO, PPC, content, design) without six hires
- Exposure to what's working across dozens of other firms
- Tools and ad platform relationships most single firms can't justify buying alone

Many growth-stage firms land on a hybrid: one in-house marketing lead who manages practice-area messaging and case intake handoff, paired with an agency for execution. That structure keeps a decision-maker inside the firm while outsourcing the labor-intensive work of running campaigns.

## The Vetting Process: Questions That Separate Signal From Pitch

Vetting a legal marketing agency should look more like due diligence than a sales call. Ask for specifics, not philosophy.

- **Show me a client in my practice area, with real numbers.** Not a logo slide — actual cost per lead and cost per case, even ranges.
- **Who owns my [SEO strategy](/blog/law-firm-seo-strategy) and PPC accounts if we leave?** If the answer isn't "you do," walk away. Ad accounts and analytics properties should be firm-owned from day one.
- **What's your churn rate, and why do clients leave?** Every agency has attrition. The honest ones can explain it.
- **How do you report on [marketing ROI](/blog/law-firm-marketing-roi), and how often?** Monthly PDFs with vanity metrics are a red flag; live dashboards tied to signed cases are the standard to expect.
- **What happens in month one versus month six?** SEO and content compound slowly. Any agency promising fast organic results is either overpromising or planning to lean entirely on paid spend.

Run this same list against two or three agencies in parallel. The differences in how specifically they answer will tell you more than any single pitch deck.

## Law Firm Marketing Agency Red Flags

Some patterns show up often enough to treat as disqualifying on their own.

- **Locked-down accounts.** If ad platforms, analytics, or your Google Business Profile are set up under the agency's master account rather than yours, you don't own your own data — they do.
- **Vague reporting.** "Impressions" and "engagement" without a line to leads and cases isn't reporting, it's activity theater.
- **Multi-year lock-in with heavy exit penalties.** Confidence in results shouldn't require a contract that punishes you for leaving.
- **One person doing everything.** SEO, ads, content, and design each require distinct expertise. A single point of contact claiming mastery of all four is a staffing problem waiting to surface.
- **No practice-area specificity.** Personal injury, mass tort, and family law have entirely different buyer behavior and sales cycles. An agency treating all legal verticals identically hasn't done the work.

## Making the Switch Without Losing Momentum

Firms that already have an agency and are evaluating a change worry, reasonably, about a dip in performance during transition. The risk is real but manageable with the right sequencing: audit current account access and ownership before signing anything new, migrate analytics and ad accounts in parallel rather than after cancellation, and hold the outgoing agency to a 30-60 day handoff window written into the original contract if possible.

The firms that get burned are usually the ones who signed with the first agency they talked to and never wrote an exit clause, because they didn't expect to need one. Every vendor relationship — marketing agency included — should be evaluated with the same scrutiny you'd apply to a [data or lead vendor](/blog/legal-lead-generation-companies): what do you own, what do you lose if it ends, and can you verify the results independently.

## The Bottom Line

A law firm marketing agency should function as an extension of your growth strategy, not a black box that produces invoices and occasional case wins. The firms that get the most out of these relationships treat vetting as seriously as they'd treat due diligence on any other six-figure vendor contract: verified account ownership, specific case-study numbers, and reporting tied to signed cases rather than clicks.

Marketing spend only pays off when the cases it produces are ones your firm can actually convert and staff. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a law firm marketing agency cost per month?

Most full-service law firm marketing agencies charge between $8,000 and $30,000 per month, separate from ad spend, depending on scope and firm size. Specialist agencies handling only SEO or only paid search often run lower, in the $2,500-$10,000 range. Performance and hybrid models shift cost based on lead volume or ad spend rather than a flat fee.

### Should a law firm build an in-house marketing team instead of hiring an agency?

It depends on scale and specialization needs rather than cost alone. Firms with one or two practice areas and a stable case volume often do well with a lean in-house lead paired with agency execution, while firms scaling across multiple markets typically need the broader bench an agency provides. The in-house vs agency decision should track your growth stage, not just your budget.

### What questions should I ask before hiring a legal marketing agency?

Ask who owns your ad accounts and analytics if the relationship ends, request practice-area-specific case studies with real cost-per-case numbers, and clarify reporting cadence and metrics upfront. Also ask about churn rate and typical client tenure — an agency that can't explain its attrition honestly is worth a second look.

### What are common law firm marketing agency red flags?

The biggest red flags are agency-owned ad accounts, vague reporting built around impressions rather than cases, and multi-year contracts with steep exit penalties. A single generalist handling SEO, paid media, content, and design simultaneously is also a sign the agency is understaffed for what it's selling.

### How long does it take to see results from a law firm marketing agency?

Paid search can generate leads within the first few weeks, but SEO and content marketing typically take three to six months to show meaningful ranking movement, per data from the [Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute.html). Any agency promising fast organic rankings is either misrepresenting the timeline or planning to rely on paid spend to mask it.
