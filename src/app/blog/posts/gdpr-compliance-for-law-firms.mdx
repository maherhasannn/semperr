A firm in Ohio with no European office, no EU clients on retainer, and no intention of ever opening one can still be squarely inside the reach of European data protection law. That surprises most managing partners, but **GDPR compliance for law firms** isn't determined by where the firm sits. It's determined by whose data the firm processes and why.

Mass tort practices pulling in claimants from EU nationals, immigration firms handling foreign national records, and cross-border corporate litigation teams all touch this exposure regularly, often without realizing it. The penalties are not theoretical: EU regulators have issued fines up to 4% of global annual revenue for serious violations, and enforcement against professional services firms has picked up sharply since 2023, a shift the [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/) has tracked closely across its cross-border privacy coverage.

## Why GDPR Compliance for Law Firms Extends Beyond Europe

The General Data Protection Regulation applies based on the location of the data subject, not the location of the firm processing their information. Article 3 of the regulation extends its reach to any organization that processes personal data of people in the EU, provided the processing relates to offering them goods or services, or to monitoring their behavior.

For a law firm, this triggers in more scenarios than most compliance teams expect:

- Representing a plaintiff who is an EU resident in a US product liability or mass tort case
- Handling discovery material that includes personal data of EU-based witnesses or employees
- Operating a client intake website that markets services to visitors physically located in the EU
- Managing corporate clients with EU subsidiaries whose employee or customer data flows through the engagement

Firms often assume that because the litigation itself is a US matter, US law governs the data. That assumption is wrong. The nationality or residency of the data subject is what matters, and it travels with the data regardless of where the case is filed.

## Who Falls Under GDPR: The Long-Arm Reach of Article 3

There's a meaningful distinction between "controller" and "processor" under the regulation, and most firms sit in both roles at different points in a matter. When a firm decides why and how client data is collected and used, it acts as a controller. When it processes data on behalf of a corporate client under that client's instructions, it acts as a processor.

This distinction changes the paperwork required. Controllers need a documented legal basis for every category of processing. Processors need a data processing agreement with the controller that spells out security obligations, subprocessor use, and breach notification timelines. Firms that skip this step because "it's just litigation support" are the ones that get caught flat-footed during a regulatory inquiry, a gap the [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/) has flagged repeatedly in its guidance on international client data handling.

## The Legal Bases Law Firms Actually Rely On

GDPR requires a lawful basis before any personal data can be processed. For law firms, three bases cover the overwhelming majority of use cases:

1. **Legitimate interest** — pursuing or defending legal claims, which GDPR explicitly recognizes as a valid basis under Article 6(1)(f) and Article 9(2)(f) for special category data.
2. **Consent** — used for marketing communications, intake forms, and anything outside the scope of the underlying legal matter.
3. **Contractual necessity** — applicable when processing is required to perform the engagement itself, such as fee agreements or conflict checks.

The mistake firms make most often is relying on consent when legitimate interest would actually be the stronger and more durable basis. Consent can be withdrawn at any time; legitimate interest, properly documented, survives a data subject's change of mind because it's tied to the firm's legal obligation to represent its client.

## Data Subject Rights and What They Mean for Client Files

GDPR gives individuals rights that don't exist under most US privacy frameworks, and firms need a process for handling them before a request arrives, not after.

### Right to Access and Erasure Requests

A data subject can request a copy of all personal data a firm holds about them, and separately, request its deletion. For litigation files, the right to erasure is not absolute. Data held to establish, exercise, or defend legal claims is generally exempt, but the firm needs to be able to articulate that exemption in writing when the request comes in, not scramble to justify it after the 30-day statutory response window has already started running.

Firms without a documented data subject request procedure typically miss this deadline entirely, which itself becomes a separate compliance failure independent of the underlying data handling.

### Right to Rectification and Data Portability

Less common in litigation contexts but still relevant for corporate and immigration practices, these rights require firms to correct inaccurate records on request and, in narrower cases, provide data in a portable, machine-readable format.

## Cross-Border Data Transfer Rules After Schrems II

The 2020 *Schrems II* decision invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield and reset the rules for moving EU personal data to US servers. Firms using US-based case management platforms, cloud document storage, or e-discovery vendors that store data domestically need a valid transfer mechanism, typically Standard Contractual Clauses, layered with a transfer impact assessment.

This matters more than firms realize because most legal case management software and litigation support platforms are hosted in US data centers by default. A firm that never asked its vendor where EU client data physically resides is very likely non-compliant without knowing it. Reviewing vendor data residency and contractual terms belongs in the same due diligence process firms should already be running when evaluating any [legal case management software](/blog/legal-case-management-software) or third-party data provider.

## Building a GDPR Compliance Program: A Practical Checklist

A defensible program doesn't require a European-style compliance department. It requires documentation that shows the firm thought about the exposure and addressed it deliberately.

- **Data mapping**: Identify every system and vendor that touches EU personal data, from intake forms to e-discovery platforms.
- **Legal basis documentation**: Record which basis applies to each category of processing, updated per matter type.
- **Data processing agreements**: Execute DPAs with every vendor and subprocessor handling EU data on the firm's behalf.
- **Breach notification protocol**: GDPR requires notifying regulators within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach — build the internal escalation chain now, not during an incident.
- **Data subject request workflow**: Assign ownership, set internal deadlines ahead of the 30-day statutory window, and document exemption reasoning for litigation holds.
- **Staff training**: Intake teams handling EU-connected matters need to recognize the trigger points before data collection even begins.

This overlaps significantly with the broader [data retention policy](/blog/legal-data-retention-policy) work firms should already have in place, since erasure obligations and retention schedules are two sides of the same governance problem.

## GDPR Compliance for Law Firms vs. US State Privacy Laws

Firms that have already built compliance programs around the CCPA or the growing list of state privacy statutes sometimes assume GDPR is just a stricter version of the same thing. The overlap is real but incomplete.

GDPR requires an affirmative legal basis for every processing activity by default, while most [US state privacy laws](/blog/state-privacy-laws-for-law-firms) operate on an opt-out model that only restricts processing once a consumer objects. GDPR's breach notification window is tighter than most state requirements. And GDPR's data subject rights, particularly erasure, are broader in scope than anything in the [CCPA compliance](/blog/ccpa-compliance-law-firms) framework.

Firms building a unified privacy program should treat GDPR as the ceiling requirement and calibrate downward for US-only matters, rather than building a patchwork of state-specific processes and hoping they happen to satisfy European standards if an EU-connected matter shows up unexpectedly. The same discipline that governs [data broker compliance](/blog/data-broker-compliance-law-firms) when firms vet lead sources applies here too: know exactly where the data came from and what obligations travel with it. Industry benchmarking from the [Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) consistently shows firms with documented data governance processes converting and retaining clients at a higher rate than those without one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does GDPR apply to US law firms?

Yes, if the firm processes personal data belonging to individuals located in the EU in connection with offering services to them or monitoring their behavior. Physical location of the firm is irrelevant; the location and residency of the data subject is what triggers GDPR's extraterritorial reach under Article 3.

### What is the legal basis for processing litigation data under GDPR?

Most law firms rely on legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f), since pursuing or defending a legal claim is explicitly recognized as a valid basis, including for sensitive special category data under Article 9(2)(f). This basis is generally more durable than consent, which can be withdrawn at any time.

### How long do law firms have to respond to a GDPR data subject access request?

Firms generally have one month (30 days) to respond to a data subject access request, with a possible two-month extension for complex requests if the data subject is notified within the initial window. Missing this deadline is treated as a separate compliance failure independent of the underlying data handling.

### Can EU clients request deletion of their litigation records?

Not always. The right to erasure is not absolute, and data held to establish, exercise, or defend legal claims is typically exempt. Firms still need a documented process to evaluate each erasure request and record the exemption basis in writing.

### Is Standard Contractual Clauses enough for transferring EU client data to US servers?

Standard Contractual Clauses are necessary but not sufficient on their own. Since the *Schrems II* ruling, firms also need a transfer impact assessment evaluating whether the destination country's laws undermine the protections the clauses are meant to provide, particularly around government access to data.
